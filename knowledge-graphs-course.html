Foundations: how knowledge graphs work

The concepts, formalisms, and techniques that every knowledge graph system draws on, independent of any one product.

M1Survey §1, Appendix A

What a knowledge graph is, and why graphs

You will be able to
  • State an inclusive definition of a knowledge graph and explain each part of it.
  • Explain why graphs suit diverse, evolving data better than a fixed relational schema.
  • Distinguish open knowledge graphs from enterprise knowledge graphs.

The phrase "knowledge graph" appears in the literature as early as 1972, but its modern use comes from the Google Knowledge Graph, announced in 2012. Airbnb, Amazon, eBay, Facebook, IBM, LinkedIn, Microsoft, Uber and others followed with their own. The ideas behind it are older: semantic networks (1950s–60s), frames, description logics, the Semantic Web (2001), and Linked Data (2006).

A working definition

The survey adopts an inclusive definition:

Definition. A knowledge graph is a graph of data intended to accumulate and convey knowledge of the real world, whose nodes represent entities of interest and whose edges represent relations between these entities.

Three ideas are packed into that sentence:

  • Graph of data (data graph). The data follows a graph-based model: a directed edge-labelled graph, a property graph, or another variant (Module 2).
  • Knowledge. "Something that is known." It can be a simple statement ("Santiago is the capital of Chile"), stored as an edge, or a quantified statement ("all capitals are cities"), which needs a more expressive representation such as an ontology or rules.
  • Accumulate. Knowledge can come from external sources, or be derived from the graph itself by deductive methods (logic: "Santiago is a city") or inductive methods (patterns and machine learning).

The Practitioner's Guide gives a complementary, business-oriented definition: a knowledge graph is "a purposeful arrangement of data such that information is put in context and insight is readily available," with an organizing principle that gives the data meaning (Module 14).

Why use a graph at all?

The survey motivates graphs with the tourism board's first attempt at a relational table, Event(name, venue, type, start, end). Real data quickly breaks it: events have several names (in different languages), several venues, several types, and unknown dates. After repeated, costly remodelling the board ends up with:

EventName(id, name)   EventStart(id, start)   EventEnd(id, end)
EventVenue(id, venue) EventType(id, type)

That flexible schema is just a set of binary relations between entities, which is a graph. Starting with a graph would have avoided the upfront schema entirely. Graphs bring several benefits:

  • Concise, intuitive abstraction for social networks, biology, citations, transport, and other connected domains, including cycles.
  • Postponed schema. Data and its scope can evolve, and incomplete knowledge is easy to capture: just omit an edge.
  • Navigational queries. Graph query languages add recursive, arbitrary-length path matching on top of joins, unions, and projections.
  • Formal semantics. Ontologies and rules can define what the labels mean and support reasoning.
  • Analytics and machine learning can run directly over the graph (centrality, clustering, embeddings, graph neural networks).

Schema, identity, and context

Knowledge graphs are usually assembled from many sources, so they vary in structure and granularity. Three representations manage that diversity: a schema gives high-level structure; identity says which nodes refer to the same real-world entity; and context records the setting in which a fact holds. Modules 4–6 cover each.

Open and enterprise knowledge graphs

Open knowledge graphs
Published online for the public good. Cross-domain examples include DBpedia, Freebase, Wikidata, and YAGO, which are extracted from Wikipedia or built by volunteers. Domain-specific ones exist for media, government, geography, tourism, and life sciences.
Enterprise knowledge graphs
Internal to a company. Used in web search (Google, Bing), commerce (Airbnb, Amazon, eBay, Uber), social networks (Facebook, LinkedIn), and finance (Accenture, Banca d'Italia, Bloomberg, Capital One, Wells Fargo) for search, recommendations, agents, advertising, analytics, and risk.

Planned applications: a multilingual tourism portal; demographic insights; sentiment analysis of reviews and complaints; tourist trajectories; new strategic flight and bus routes; and personalised recommendations. Graph data models

You will be able to
  • Describe directed edge-labelled graphs (and RDF), heterogeneous graphs, property graphs, and graph datasets.
  • Choose a model by weighing minimality against flexibility.
  • Name the main ways to store graphs.

Directed edge-labelled graphs (del graphs)

A set of nodes (Santiago, EID16, 2018-03-22 12:00) and a set of directed, labelled edges between them, such as Santa Lucía —city→ Santiago. Also called a multi-relational graph. Adding information means adding nodes and edges; incomplete information means leaving an edge out (the Food Truck festival simply has no start date yet).

graph LR
  EID15((EID15)) -- name --> Nam["Ñam"]
  EID15 -- type --> FF[Food Festival]
  EID15 -- venue --> SL[Santa Lucía]
  SL -- city --> SCL[Santiago]
  EID16((EID16)) -- name --> FT["Food Truck"]
  EID16 -- type --> FF
  EID16 -- venue --> SO[Sotomayor]
  SO -- city --> VM[Viña del Mar]
  SCL -- flight --> ARI[Arica]
  ARI -- bus --> VM
  VM -- bus --> SCL

Compared with trees (XML, JSON), graphs don't force a hierarchy (should venue be a parent or sibling of type?) and they represent cycles naturally.

RDF

The W3C's Resource Description Framework standardises del graphs. RDF distinguishes three node types:

  • IRIs (Internationalized Resource Identifiers) for globally identifying entities on the Web.
  • Literals for strings (with optional language tags) and typed values such as integers and dates.
  • Blank nodes: anonymous nodes with no identifier, usable instead of internal IDs like EID15.

Heterogeneous graphs

Every node and edge has exactly one type, and the node type is part of the model rather than a type edge. An edge between same-typed nodes (borders between countries) is homogeneous; between different types (capital from Country to City) it is heterogeneous. Partitioning by type helps machine learning, but each node gets only one type, whereas a del graph allows zero or many.

Property graphs

Property graphs attach a label and a set of property–value pairs to both nodes and edges. Suppose the board wants to record which company operates each flight. In a del graph you cannot annotate the edge Santiago —flight→ Arica directly; you must turn the flight into a node (LA380) with from, to, company, and mode edges. In a property graph the edge itself carries company = LATAM, and nodes carry lat and long.

graph LR
  S["Santiago : Capital City<br/>lat = −33.45, long = −70.66"] -- "LA380 : flight<br/>company = LATAM" --> A["Arica : Port City<br/>lat = −18.48, long = −70.33"]
  A -- "LA381 : flight<br/>company = LATAM" --> S

Property graphs are the model behind Neo4j and are used in Part II. Property graphs and del graphs can be translated into each other without loss of information. The survey sums up the trade-off: del graphs are more minimal, property graphs more flexible, and the choice often comes down to which implementations are available.

Graph datasets

A graph dataset is a set of named graphs (each a pair of graph ID and graph) plus a default graph with no ID. You might keep Events and Routes in separate named graphs and store metadata about them (such as when each was last updated) in the default graph. This lets you update one source independently, separate trustworthy from untrustworthy sources, and track provenance. Graph names can themselves appear as nodes. Linked Data on the Web is a common use case.

Other models

Some models allow complex nodes containing edges or nested graphs (hypernodes); hypergraphs have edges that connect sets of nodes. Data can usually be converted between models.

M3Survey §2.2

Querying graphs

You will be able to
  • Evaluate a basic graph pattern and explain homomorphism vs isomorphism semantics.
  • Combine patterns with relational operators into complex graph patterns.
  • Write regular path queries with inverse, Kleene star, disjunction, and concatenation.

The main graph query languages are SPARQL (for RDF) and Cypher, Gremlin, and G-CORE (for property graphs). They share a few primitives.

Basic graph patterns

A graph pattern looks like the data graph but allows variables (written ?event) alongside constants. Evaluating it produces mappings from variables to constants such that, after substitution, the pattern is contained in the data graph. The result is a table.

Pattern: ?ev —type→ Food Festival, ?ev —venue→ ?vn1, ?ev —venue→ ?vn2. Over the tourism graph it returns rows such as (EID16, Piscina Olímpica, Sotomayor), and also rows where ?vn1 and ?vn2 map to the same venue.

Whether those "same venue" rows count depends on the semantics:

  • Homomorphism-based: different variables may map to the same term. SPARQL uses this.
  • Isomorphism-based: variables must map to distinct terms. Cypher applies it to edges (a relationship is not reused within one match).

Complex graph patterns

Since a pattern yields a table, relational algebra can transform and combine results:

OperatorEffect
Projection πKeep some columns (variables)
Selection σKeep rows matching a condition
Renaming ρRename columns
Union ∪, difference −Merge or subtract result tables
Join ⋈Extend rows with compatible rows from another table
Anti-join ▷ (NOT EXISTS)Rows with no compatible partner
Left-join ⟕ (OPTIONAL)Join but keep unmatched rows

Graph patterns with projection equal conjunctive queries; with the full set of operators they equal first-order queries. Example: "food or drinks festivals not held in Santiago, optionally with their start date and name."

Results can contain duplicates. Bag semantics keeps them; set semantics (the DISTINCT keyword) removes them.

Navigational graph patterns

The feature that sets graph query languages apart is the path expression: a regular expression over edge labels, used in a regular path query (x, r, y).

  • p: an edge label
  • r⁻: inverse (traverse backwards)
  • r*: Kleene star, zero or more repetitions
  • r1 | r2: disjunction
  • r1 · r2: concatenation
(Arica, (bus | flight)*, ?city) finds every city reachable from Arica by bus or flight. Combined with ?event —type→ Food Festival and (venue · city)⁻, it finds food festivals in cities reachable from Arica.

Cycles make the set of matching paths infinite, so languages restrict what they return: shortest paths only, paths without repeated nodes or edges (Cypher), or simply the pairs of connected nodes (SPARQL 1.1).

Other features

Aggregation (GROUP BY, COUNT), datatype filters, federation across remote graphs, update languages, and entailment regimes.

# SPARQL: names of food festivals and their venues' cities
SELECT ?name ?city WHERE {
  ?ev a :FoodFestival ; :name ?name ; :venue/:city ?city .
}
// Cypher: same idea on a property graph
MATCH (e:FoodFestival)-[:VENUE]->(:Venue)-[:CITY]->(c:City)
RETURN e.name, c.name

Schema: semantic, validating, emergent

You will be able to
  • Use sub-class, sub-property, domain, and range to infer new edges (RDFS).
  • Explain the Open World, Closed World, and Local Closed World Assumptions.
  • Describe shapes (SHACL, ShEx), open vs closed shapes, and quotient graphs.

A graph lets you postpone a schema, but you still often want one. The survey distinguishes three kinds.

1. Semantic schema: meaning, for inference

Defines the meaning of vocabulary terms. Classes such as Event, Festival, and Food Festival form a class hierarchy; properties form a property hierarchy (venue and city are sub-properties of location; bus and flight of connects to).

FeatureDefinitionInferenceExample
Subclassc subc. of dx type c ⇒ x type dCity subc. of Place
Subpropertyp subp. of qx p y ⇒ x q yvenue subp. of location
Domainp domain cx p y ⇒ x type cvenue domain Event
Rangep range cx p y ⇒ y type cvenue range Venue

The W3C standard for this is RDF Schema (RDFS), whose definitions are themselves serialised as a graph. OWL goes much further (Module 7).

Open vs closed world
  • Closed World Assumption (CWA): the data is complete; what is not stated is false. Typical of classical databases.
  • Open World Assumption (OWA): a missing edge only means "unknown." A missing Viña del Mar —flight→ Arica doesn't prove no flight exists.
  • Local Closed World Assumption (LCWA): parts of the graph are declared complete (for example, the board knows all flights), so the system can answer "no" there.

Under CWA, adding an edge can contradict a previous "false"; under OWA, something proven false stays false as edges are added.

2. Validating schema: constraints, for checking

Where semantic schemas infer new data, validating schemas check existing data, for example that every event has a name, a venue, a start, and an end. The standard approach is shapes. A shape targets nodes (instances of a class, subjects of a property, results of a query…) and states constraints on them: cardinalities such as [1..*] or [1..1], value types (string, dateTime), and links to nodes conforming to other shapes.

Event:  name: string [1..*]   start: dateTime [1..1]   end: dateTime [1..1]
        type: any [1..*]      venue → Venue [1..*]
Venue:  indoor: boolean [0..1]   city → City [0..1]   (inherits Place)
City:   population: int ∧ >5000 [0..1]               (inherits Place)
Place:  lat, long: float [0..1]   flight, bus → Place [0..*]

Here EID15 conforms to Event, which depends on Santa Lucía conforming to Venue and Santiago to City. Conformance can be recursive. EID16 fails because it lacks start and end.

  • Open shapes allow extra properties; closed shapes forbid them. If you add Santiago —founder→ Pedro de Valdivia, Santiago conforms to City only if City is open.
  • Shapes can combine with and, or, not. Mixing recursion and negation can cause paradoxes (a "Barber" shape modelled on the barber paradox), which stratification, partial assignments, or stable-model semantics resolve.
  • The two RDF shape languages are SHACL (W3C Recommendation) and ShEx (W3C Community Group Report).
  • Validation can run over the graph after inference from a semantic schema; this may require relaxed cardinalities or open shapes.

3. Emergent schema: structure, discovered

Latent structure can be extracted automatically as an emergent schema (or graph summary). A common framework is the quotient graph: partition nodes (event, name, venue, class, date-time, city), merge each partition into one node, and keep an edge X→Z if some member of X links to some member of Z.

  • Every quotient graph simulates its input, but may overstate (it suggests EID16 has a start date).
  • A bisimilar quotient also requires every member of X to have the edge; it splits events with and without dates. Bisimilarity preserves forward path queries.

Uses: a human-readable overview, a starting point for semantic or validating schemas, query optimisation, and integration.

Identity: which Santiago?

You will be able to
  • Explain naming clashes and how persistent identifiers and IRIs prevent them.
  • Use owl:sameAs and identifying properties to ground identity.
  • Work with datatypes, labels, and existential (blank) nodes.

The node Santiago could mean Santiago de Chile, Santiago de Cuba, Santiago de Compostela, or an indie rock band. Context helps, but heuristics fail in harder cases.

Persistent identifiers

Merge a Chilean and a Cuban tourism graph that both use the local name Santiago, and the result says one Santiago is in both countries: a naming clash. Persistent identifiers (PIDs) solve this: DOIs for papers, ORCID iDs for authors, ISBNs for books, Alpha-2 codes for countries.

RDF goes further and uses global IRIs. It separates the identifier of a thing from the URL of the page that describes it. In Wikidata, https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Q2887 is the web page about Santiago, while http://www.wikidata.org/entity/Q2887 is the city itself. Without that split, "founded by Pedro de Valdivia" is ambiguous: founder of the page, or of the city?

  • Namespaces and prefixes abbreviate IRIs: wd:Q2887 wdt:P112 wd:Q203534.
  • Dereferenceable HTTP IRIs return descriptions of the entity, which enables Linked Data (Module 12).
  • PURL services redirect a stable identifier to a changing location, improving persistence.
  • URIs include URLs (locate) and URNs (name); IRIs are URIs that allow Unicode (http://example.com/Ñam).

External identity links

IRIs avoid clashes but don't establish that two IRIs mean the same thing: chile:Santiago and geo:SantiagoDeChile stay distinct after a merge. Two remedies:

  1. Add uniquely identifying information: coordinates, postal code, founding year.
  2. Add an identity link: chile:Santiago owl:sameAs geo:SantiagoDeChile. OWL semantics then let you combine the data of both nodes.

Datatypes

Values like dates are identified by their syntax, not by an IRI. RDF uses XML Schema Datatypes: a literal is a pair of lexical string and datatype IRI, such as "2020-03-29T20:00:00"^^xsd:dateTime. Without a datatype, xsd:string is assumed. Literals cannot have outgoing edges. Applications can order, compare, and cast typed values. Neo4j has its own types: numbers, strings, booleans, spatial points, temporal values.

Lexicalisation

Identifiers like wd:Q2887 are deliberately opaque: they stay stable when names change (Eswatini, formerly Swaziland, kept wd:Q1050) and favour no language. Human-readable labels are added as edges: rdfs:label "Santiago", skos:altLabel "Santiago de Chile", rdfs:comment, with language tags like "Ciudad"@es. Graphs rich in such labels are (multilingual) lexicalised knowledge graphs.

Existential nodes

Two events share a venue that hasn't been announced. Omitting the venue loses information; inventing an IRI pretends the venue is known. An existential node (RDF blank node) says "some venue exists" without naming it. Blank nodes also build structures like RDF lists (rdf:first, rdf:rest, rdf:nil). They complicate comparing graphs, so they can be skolemised (replaced by canonical labels) or avoided.

M6Survey §3.3

Context: when and where a fact holds

You will be able to
  • Define context as the scope of truth and name its common kinds.
  • Compare direct representation, three reification patterns, and higher-arity options.
  • Explain annotation frameworks with meet and join operators.

Most facts are true only in some context. Temporal: Santiago has been a city since 1541; flights Arica–Santiago began in 1956. Geographic: the graph describes Chile. Provenance: EID15's data came from the Ñam webpage on 4 January 2020. Contexts combine: Arica is a Chilean city (geographic) since 1883 (temporal) according to the Treaty of Ancón (provenance). Making context explicit lets you ask what held in 2016, or what holds if you exclude unreliable sources. Context can sit on nodes, edges, or sub-graphs.

Direct representation

Treat context as ordinary data: the event's start and end dates, or turn the flight edge into a flight node. Standard vocabularies help: the Time Ontology (instants, intervals, before, overlaps) and the PROV Data Model (entities derived from entities, generated or used by activities, attributed to agents).

Reification: edges about edges

To say Santiago —flight→ Arica is valid from 1956, create an identifier e for the edge itself:

PatternStructure
RDF reificatione —subject→ Santiago, e —predicate→ flight, e —object→ Arica, e —valid from→ 1956
n-ary relationSantiago —flight→ e —value→ Arica, e —valid from→ 1956
Singleton propertySantiago —e→ Arica, e —singleton→ flight, e —valid from→ 1956

A reified edge does not assert the edge itself, so you can reify an edge to say it is no longer valid.

Higher-arity representations

  • Named graph: put the edge in a graph and annotate the graph. Most flexible: many edges can share one context.
  • Property graph: put valid from = 1956 on the edge.
  • RDF* (RDF-star): an edge can be used as a node. Least flexible without edge IDs: you cannot pair "2006–2010" and "2014–2018" for Bachelet's two presidencies on one edge.

Annotations: reasoning with context

Annotations define a contextual domain mathematically so that it can be computed automatically. Temporal RDF annotates edges with intervals; Fuzzy RDF with degrees of truth (Santiago has a semi-arid climate, degree 0.8). Annotated RDF is domain-independent, using semirings with two operators:

  • meet: combine annotations that must hold together (intersection of day sets).
  • join: combine alternative results (union of day sets).
Query: flights from Santiago to cities with events. A Punta Arenas event on days 150–152 doesn't intersect the flight days [1–120, 220–365], so it drops out. Arica has two events whose meets give days 123–125 and 276–279; joined, Arica is returned with {[123,125],[276,279]}.

Other frameworks

Contextual knowledge repositories assign sub-graphs a value on each context dimension, with partial orders (2020-03-22 ⪯ 2020-03 ⪯ 2020). Contextual OLAP builds a data cube whose cells contain knowledge graphs, with slice-and-dice and roll-up.

M7Survey §4

Deductive knowledge: ontologies, entailment, reasoning

You will be able to
  • Explain interpretations, models, and entailment, with UNA/NUNA and OWA/CWA.
  • Read the main OWL features for individuals, properties, and classes.
  • Compare reasoning by materialisation, query rewriting, and Description Logics.

Humans deduce that the Ñam festival is located in Santiago, or that cities with flights have airports nearby. Machines need explicit premises and entailment regimes. Then they deduce with precision and scale, which helps query answering, classification, and finding inconsistencies.

The query "names of festivals with location Santiago" returns nothing over the raw graph: no node is typed Festival, and nothing has a direct location edge. With "Food Festival ⊑ Festival" and "venue then city implies location," the answer Ñam is entailed.

Ontologies

What is an "event"? One venue or many? One time interval or many? There is no single correct answer; it is convention. An ontology is a concrete, formal representation of what terms mean within a scope. Its value depends on agreement, detail, and adoption; shared ontologies improve interoperability. The main languages are OWL (W3C, works with RDF; largely subsumes RDFS) and OBOF (biomedicine).

Interpretations and assumptions

An interpretation maps the data graph's nodes and edge labels to a domain graph of entities and relations. Assumptions decide which interpretations count:

  • UNA (Unique Name Assumption): different names mean different entities, so Arica→Santiago and Viña del Mar→Santiago are at least two flights.
  • NUNA: two names may denote one entity, so there is at least one flight.
  • OWL adopts NUNA and OWA, the most general case.

Semantics and entailment

Interpretations that satisfy a graph are its models. Each axiom restricts the models. Graph G entails G′ if every model of G is a model of G′. Example: Santiago type City plus City subc. of Place entails Santiago type Place.

If–then vs if-and-only-if semantics. nearby type Symmetric and nearby inv. of nearby impose the same condition. Under if-and-only-if semantics (used by OWL) they entail each other; under if–then semantics they don't.

Reasoning

Deciding entailment for all OWL features is undecidable. Practical reasoners give up one property:

  1. Always halt but may miss entailments (incomplete). Scalable; good with incomplete data. Usually rules.
  2. Always halt and are complete, but accept only restricted features. Preferred where missed entailments are dangerous, such as medicine. Usually Description Logics.
  3. Always correct but may never halt (first-order theorem provers).
Rules

A rule has a body (if) and head (then), both graph patterns. It corresponds to Datalog or Horn clauses.

?x type ?c . ?c subc. of ?d     ⇒  ?x type ?d
?x ?p ?y   . ?p subp. of ?q     ⇒  ?x ?q ?y
?x ?p ?y   . ?p domain ?c       ⇒  ?x type ?c
?x ?p ?y   . ?p range ?c        ⇒  ?y type ?c
?x flight ?y . ?x country ?z . ?y country ?z  ⇒  ?x domestic flight ?y

The last rule infers from a cycle, which ontology features cannot express. OWL 2 RL/RDF is a (necessarily incomplete) rule set for OWL. Rule languages include N3, RIF, SWRL, and SPIN; Datalog± adds existentials and Disjunctive Datalog adds disjunction.

  • Materialisation: apply rules repeatedly, adding conclusions, until a fixpoint. Optimised by Rete networks or MapReduce; the result can get very large.
  • Query rewriting: expand the query instead. ?x type Event becomes ?x type Event ∪ ?x type Festival ∪ ?x type Periodic Market ∪ ?x venue ?y. The OWL 2 QL profile is designed for this.
Description Logics

DLs formalised frames and semantic networks and heavily influenced OWL; OWL 2 DL is the decidable fragment. They describe individuals, classes (concepts), and properties (roles):

  • A-Box (assertions): City(Santiago), flight(Santiago, Arica).
  • T-Box (class axioms): City ⊑ Place; ∃flight.⊤ ⊑ ∃nearby.Airport; Airport ⊑ DomesticAirport ⊔ InternationalAirport.
  • R-Box (property axioms): flight ⊑ connectsTo.

Expressive DLs reduce entailment to (un)satisfiability and use tableau methods, which branch on disjunctions and introduce elements for existentials. Too costly for large data, but valuable for modelling complex domains.

Frameworks

Apache Spark GraphX, GraphLab, Pregel, Signal–Collect, and Shark use a systolic (vertex-centric) abstraction: each node is a processor. In every iteration a node reads incoming messages (Msg phase), aggregates them (Agg phase), and sends results along its out-edges.

PageRank: each of n nodes starts at 1/n. A node sends d·R(v)/|out-edges| to each neighbour (damping factor d ≈ 0.85). Each node sums its messages and adds (1−d)/n. Repeat until convergence. The score is the probability a randomly travelling tourist is at that place.

Message passing can't express everything (it is as powerful as the Weisfeiler–Lehman test), so frameworks add global steps and mutation steps.

Running analytics on data graphs

Most algorithms expect plain directed or undirected graphs. Four strategies prepare a data graph:

  • Projection: select a sub-graph (bus and flight edges) and drop labels.
  • Weighting: turn edge data into numbers (trip duration, price).
  • Transformation: convert to a lower-arity graph, lossy or lossless (lossless adds nodes, like reification).
  • Customisation: adapt the algorithm to use labels.

Results can change dramatically between strategies. Analytics also combine with queries (extract a sub-graph, compute its strongly connected component, query events outside it during an airline strike) and with entailment: hosts and inverse venue edges mean the same thing but change direction-sensitive analytics. Semantically invariant analytics remain an open research topic.

Knowledge graph embeddings

Machine learning wants dense vectors. One-hot encoding a graph gives huge, sparse vectors (|L|·|V| long). An embedding maps each entity to a vector e and each relation to r in a low-dimensional space (typically 50–1000 dimensions). A scoring function rates the plausibility of an edge s–p→o. Training maximises the score of positive edges (in the graph) and minimises it for negative ones (edges with a node or label swapped out). This is self-supervision.

Uses: confidence for extracted edges, link prediction (Module 11), similarity for deduplication and recommendation.

Graph neural networks

A GNN builds a neural network shaped like the data graph and learns, supervised, to map node features to outputs. It supports end-to-end classification of nodes, edges, or whole graphs.

  • Recursive GNNs (Scarselli et al.): each node has fixed feature vectors and a state vector. A learned transition function updates the state from neighbours' features and states, and an output function produces the result. Iterate to a fixpoint (the transition must be a contraction). Example: learn where to place tourist information offices from labelled examples.
  • Convolutional GNNs: the transition function is a convolution, as in image CNNs. Neighbourhood sizes vary, so they use spectral or spatial representations, or attention. They run a fixed number of layers with different weights per layer, while RecGNNs reuse one function to a fixpoint.

Symbolic learning

Numeric models are hard to explain and suffer from the out-of-vocabulary problem: a new node (CDG) has no embedding until retraining. Symbolic learning produces interpretable, quantified rules and axioms that apply to unseen nodes and can be checked by experts.

Rule mining
  • Support: number of a rule's entailments that are positive (in the graph).
  • Confidence: ratio of entailments that are positive.
  • Partial Completeness Assumption (PCA): an edge x–p→y′ counts as negative only if x has some other p-edge. PCA confidence = support ÷ (positive + negative entailments).
Rule ?x flight ?y ⇒ ?y flight ?x over a small airport graph: support 4, confidence 4/5 = 0.8, because SCL→ARI is a PCA negative (SCL has another flight, to LIM).

AMIE mines rules top-down from a head, adding body edges by three refinements (fresh variable, constant, or two existing variables), pruning by support, and keeping only closed rules (every variable appears at least twice). Later systems learn exceptions (non-monotonic rules), use embeddings (RuLES), cardinalities (CARL), or ontology-entailed negatives. Differentiable rule mining (NeuralLP, DRUM) treats rule bodies as adjacency-matrix multiplications and learns path-like rules end to end.

Axiom mining

Learns DL axioms such as disjointness (DomesticAirport ⊓ InternationalAirport ≡ ⊥) via association rules or TF-IDF class similarity. DL-Learner learns class descriptions separating positive from negative examples, e.g. {Iquique, Arica} vs {Santiago} gives ∃nearby.Airport ⊓ ¬(∃capital⁻.⊤).

Knowledge graphs are usually built from a small core and enriched incrementally ("pay-as-you-go", agile). The tourism board starts with attractions and events, and adds routes and crime reports later.

Human collaboration

In-house editors, crowdsourcing, feedback, or wikis. Some major graphs (Wikidata, early Freebase) rely mostly on people. Costs are high, and problems include error, disagreement, bias, and vandalism, plus licensing, tooling, and culture. People are also used to verify extracted data, write mappings, and design schemas.

Text sources

Sentence: "Santiago has flights to Rapa Nui, which was named a World Heritage Site in 1995."

  1. Pre-processing: tokenisation, part-of-speech tagging, dependency parsing, word sense disambiguation (WordNet, BabelNet: "flights" as air travel, not stairs).
  2. Named Entity Recognition: find mentions of people, places, organisations. Supervised, bootstrapped from seeds, distant supervision (use entities already in the graph), or hand rules. New entities are emerging entities.
  3. Entity Linking: link mentions to existing nodes. Challenges: aliases (Rapa Nui = Easter Island) and ambiguity (which Santiago?). Rank candidates using context, prior probability, and centrality.
  4. Relation Extraction: binary relations in a closed setting (patterns, supervised, bootstrapping, distant supervision) or open (Open Information Extraction, relations taken from parse trees). n-ary RE captures context such as "in 1995," using frame semantics (FrameNet) or Discourse Representation Theory: ∃e: naming(e), patient(e, Easter Island), name(e, World Heritage Site). Open relations like "has flights to" must be aligned to existing labels (flight).

Tasks are often done jointly (WSD+EL, NER+EL, NER+RE) for better accuracy.

Markup sources

  • Wrapper-based: rules that locate data in HTML. Manual wrappers are brittle; induced wrappers (e.g. LODIE with distant supervision) learn tag paths from known edges and apply them to similar pages.
  • Web tables: classify tables (many are for layout), normalise (spans, splits, nesting), find the protagonist (main entity, often outside the table), then link cells, types, and relations. DBpedia and YAGO extract Wikipedia infoboxes.
  • Deep Web crawling: fill in search forms to reach data behind them.

Structured sources

Structured data can be mapped precisely rather than extracted imprecisely.

Tables
  • Direct mapping (W3C standard): each non-null cell becomes an edge row–column→value; rows are named from primary keys (Claimant-XY12SDA vs the raw value XY12SDA); foreign keys become links; SQL types become XSD datatypes; nulls are omitted (not blank nodes, since SQL null can mean "no value"). There is also a CSV mapping standard and one for property graphs.
  • Custom mapping with R2RML: constants, cell values, templates ({id}-{country}), or SQL queries, e.g. a GROUP BY count producing U.S. —crimes→ 2.
ETL (materialisation)
Transform and load the data as a graph. Use it like any other graph data, but updates must be propagated.
Query rewriting (virtualisation)
Translate graph queries (SPARQL, Cypher) into SQL over the source. One copy of the data. Ontology-Based Data Access (OBDA) adds entailment.
Trees and other graphs

Naive child-edges mirror tree structure, so use custom mappings: GRDDL for XML, JSON-LD for JSON; XSPARQL queries XML and RDF together. From other knowledge graphs (DBpedia, Wikidata, LinkedGeoData…), pull relevant sub-graphs with SPARQL CONSTRUCT and align entities and schema.

Schema and ontology creation

Ontology engineering moved from waterfall methods to agile ones: DILIGENT (local vs global changes with review, like SNOMED CT's national extensions), eXtreme Design, Modular Ontology Modelling, SAMOD. Two key elements:

  • Competency questions: natural-language questions the ontology must answer, turned into test queries. "What events happen in Santiago?" fails until location type Transitive is added.
  • Ontology Design Patterns: reusable modelling templates, e.g. the Core Event pattern (spatio-temporal extent, sub-events, participants).

Ontology learning extracts terminology from text (unithood and termhood: "visitor visa", "World Heritage Site"), sub-class axioms from noun modifiers or Hearst patterns ("discounts, such as off-peak rates" ⇒ Off-Peak Rate ⊑ Discount), and even disjointness or cardinality axioms.

Refinement: completion and correction

You will be able to
  • Distinguish general, type, and identity link prediction.
  • Explain blocking for entity matching.
  • Describe fact validation and inconsistency repair.

Refinement improves the graph as it is (completing and correcting) rather than ingesting new sources.

Completion

Knowledge graphs are incomplete. Link prediction (from Statistical Relational Learning) predicts missing edges.

  • General links: embeddings or mined rules. Since bus routes are usually return routes, predict Moon Valley —bus→ San Pedro.
  • Type links: a classification task over a few classes, using features like incoming and outgoing edge labels, or GNNs. Nodes with flight edges are likely Cities (right for Iquique, wrong for Easter Island).
  • Identity links: entity matching (record linkage, deduplication). Value matchers compare property values (population 7400 vs 7500); context matchers compare neighbourhoods (Easter Island vs Rapa Nui have similar coordinates and the same seat).

Pairwise comparison costs O(n²), so blocking groups candidates by similarity-preserving keys (geographic regions), or uses windowing or multidimensional search. Confident matches are consolidated onto a canonical node.

Correction

Fact validation (fact checking)

Score an edge's veracity, 0–1, against external sources. Unlike link prediction, it is usually online and evaluates given edges.

  • Unstructured sources: verbalise the edge into text, then use fact finders on a bipartite source–fact graph with mutually dependent trustworthiness and plausibility: Sums (HITS-based), Average Log (normalises prolific sources), Investment.
  • Structured sources: find paths in reference graphs that support the edge (mutual information, metapaths, PredPath), or use embeddings.
Inconsistency repair

If Santiago is typed City and Airport (disjoint), which edge do you remove? And removing Santiago type Airport is not enough if flight range Airport re-entails it. Options: suggest repairs for manual choice, compute minimal hitting sets weighted by source trust (Bonatti et al.), or answer queries with consistent query answering that holds under every possible repair.

Graphs, not charts

"Graph" here means nodes and relationships, not plotted charts. Graph theory began with Leonhard Euler and the seven bridges of Königsberg (18th century): Euler stripped away the geography and kept only connections, proving no walk could cross every bridge exactly once. The book compares this to software: remove real-world noise and keep the logical model.

Definition and the organizing principle

"Knowledge graphs are interlinked sets of facts that describe real-world entities, events, or things and their interrelations in a human- and machine-understandable format." Critically, they must have an organizing principle so that people and software can reason about the data. It makes the data smarter, instead of keeping intelligence in applications and treating data as dumb.

A knowledge graph is agnostic about physical storage: a single graph database, a federation of graph stores, a layer over a data lake, or a logical layer over many sources; fully materialised, virtualised ("smart index"), or hybrid. The book's summary image: a knowledge graph is like a skilled librarian who curates and recommends.

Why labels are not types. In the LPG, labels are tags describing a node's role, with no inheritance. Type systems are left to applications by design; the database owns data and structure. Inheritance-style reasoning exists in parts of the Semantic Web stack, but the authors note it is complex and can be slow or intractable.

Choosing a principle

  • Let the intended use drive it. An ambitious metamodel up front is a common, expensive mistake.
  • Don't boil the ocean: capture the business language incrementally, treat it as malleable and versioned, and deliver value early (agile).
  • Embrace just-enough semantics and avoid "ontological perfectionism."
Standard or your own?

Standards include SNOMED CT (clinical), Library of Congress Classification, FIBO (finance), Schema.org (web), and Dublin Core. Adopting one fosters interoperability and may be mandatory for regulatory reporting. If none fits, write your own: in natural language (easy but not machine readable) or in RDFS, OWL, or SKOS (taxonomies), which gives you editors and automated reasoning. Successful teams mix standards with adaptive customisation.

Graph databases and the Cypher query language

You will be able to
  • Write CREATE, MATCH, MERGE, SET, REMOVE, DELETE, and DETACH DELETE statements.
  • Avoid duplicates with MERGE and node-key constraints.
  • Write graph-local and graph-global queries, call procedures, and tune with EXPLAIN/PROFILE.
  • Explain index-free adjacency, property-access cost, and ACID with Raft.

Cypher is a declarative, pattern-matching language created by Neo4j and implemented elsewhere; its ideas feed the ISO standard GQL. Its patterns look like ASCII-art diagrams.

(:Person {name:'Rosa'})-[:LIVES_IN {since:2020}]->(:Place {city:'Berlin', country:'DE'})
 ^ node  ^ label  ^ properties  ^ relationship type    ^ direction

Labels also put nodes in a label index the planner uses, so "if you know something about your domain, put it in your knowledge graph." Direction is mandatory when storing (Rosa lives in Berlin, not the reverse) but relationships can be traversed either way at the same constant cost.

Creating and deleting

CREATE (:Person {name:'Rosa'})-[:LIVES_IN {since:2020}]->(:Place {city:'Berlin', country:'DE'})

MATCH (n) RETURN n LIMIT 25          // show some of the graph
MATCH (n) DELETE n                   // aborts if relationships would dangle
MATCH ()-[r:LIVES_IN]->() DELETE r   // delete relationships
MATCH (n) DETACH DELETE n            // delete nodes and their relationships (whole graph)

CREATE always inserts, so running it twice gives two Rosas and two Berlins.

MERGE and its trap

MERGE matches the whole pattern or creates the whole pattern. It never matches part and creates the rest. After Karl lives in London, MERGE (:Person {name:'Fred'})-[:LIVES_IN]->(:Place {city:'London', country:'UK'}) can't match fully, so it creates a second London.

The fix has two parts. First, a constraint so duplicate places are rejected (composite node keys are an Enterprise Edition feature):

CREATE CONSTRAINT no_duplicate_cities
FOR (p:Place) REQUIRE (p.country, p.city) IS NODE KEY

Second, merge piece by piece, binding variables, in one transaction:

MERGE (london:Place {city:'London', country:'UK'})
MERGE (fred:Person {name:'Fred'})
MERGE (fred)-[:LIVES_IN]->(london)
MERGE (karl:Person {name:'Karl'})
MERGE (karl)-[:LIVES_IN]->(london)

London, UK and London, Ontario can coexist; duplicates of either cannot.

MATCH (p:Person) WHERE p.name = 'Rosa' SET p.dob = 19841203   // add/update property
MATCH (p:Person) WHERE p.name = 'Rosa' REMOVE p.dob           // remove property
MATCH (p:Person) WHERE p.name = 'Rosa' REMOVE p:Person        // remove label

Graph-local queries

Anchored to known nodes.

// Who lives in Berlin?
MATCH (p:Person)-[:LIVES_IN]->(:Place {city:'Berlin', country:'DE'})
RETURN p

// Friends of friends: exactly 2 hops, excluding Rosa herself
MATCH (rosa:Person {name:'Rosa'})-[:FRIEND*2..2]->(fof:Person)
WHERE rosa <> fof
RETURN fof

// Friends or friends-of-friends of anyone living in Berlin
MATCH (:Place {city:'Berlin'})<-[:LIVES_IN]-(p:Person)<-[:FRIEND*1..2]-(f:Person)
WHERE f <> p
RETURN f

Without WHERE rosa <> fof, Rosa appears as her own friend-of-friend via Rosa→Karl→Rosa. Other predicates: STARTS WITH, CONTAINS, ENDS WITH, IN [...], and path patterns such as WHERE NOT (p)-[:KNOWS]->(:Person {name:'Karl'}).

No join bombs. Unlike recursive joins in SQL, query latency in Neo4j is proportional to how much of the graph is visited, not to database size. A single traversal costs on the order of microseconds.

Graph-global queries

Not anchored; they process much or all of the graph.

// Most popular cities to live in
MATCH (p:Place)<-[l:LIVES_IN]-(:Person)
RETURN p AS place, count(l) AS rels
ORDER BY rels DESC

Use aggregation (count, avg, max, min, sum), SKIP, and LIMIT to return compact answers from huge graphs. Tip: include labels you know (helps the planner) and never omit relationship types, which explodes the search space.

Procedures, functions, and tuning

CALL db.schema.visualization()                         // see the graph's schema

MATCH (p:Person {name:'Fred'})
CALL apoc.atomic.concat(p, "name", 'dy')               // procedure: Fred → Freddy
YIELD oldValue, newValue RETURN oldValue, newValue;

APOC is the standard library of procedures and functions that ships with Neo4j; check it before writing something complicated. EXPLAIN shows the plan without running; PROFILE runs and instruments it. Reduce "DB hits" and cardinality early; scans can indicate a missing index; minimise time even at the cost of some DB hits.

Inside Neo4j: "mechanical sympathy"

  • Index-free adjacency: graph structure is stored as fixed-length node and relationship records, so record ID × record size gives the file offset. Traversals are O(1) pointer chasing. A node's relationships act as its local index.
  • Property storage is a linked list of property records: O(N) reads, O(1) writes. Many property reads during big traversals can double latency, so traverse first, read properties last.
  • Memory hierarchy: keep the working set in RAM; for the most demanding cases, fit the whole graph in RAM. Avoid spinning disks (a 5 ms seek is thousands of traversals). Sometimes the cheapest fix is more RAM.
  • ACID transactions via a write-ahead log for crash recovery; clustering uses the Raft consensus algorithm; an opt-in causal barrier means you always read your own writes.

The generated Cypher uses three idioms: CREATE CONSTRAINT, UNWIND (turn a list into rows), and MERGE. For relationships it MATCHes nodes known to exist and MERGEs only the relationship. You can keep the generated Cypher in source control.

LOAD CSV

// Irregular data: MERGE on the key, SET optional properties (null-safe)
LOAD CSV WITH HEADERS FROM 'people.csv' AS line

SET p.gender = line.gender

Putting a possibly-null value inside a MERGE pattern fails, so set it afterwards. Within one transaction the relationship and property are written atomically. Use real unique IDs rather than names in production.

Batching large loads
LOAD CSV WITH HEADERS FROM 'people.csv' AS line
CALL {
  WITH line
  MERGE (p:Person {name: line.name})

Towards a data fabric

A data fabric is an organisation-wide data access layer that offers a connected view of underlying systems. At its heart, the knowledge graph acts as a curated index across silos: "Give me the customer John Smith from Seattle" follows relationships to leaf nodes that point to records in a document store (customers), another graph (catalogue), and a relational database (billing). The client never sees the backends. Graphs fit because they accommodate several representations of the same entity and allow incremental association without big up-front design. Overlaying an ontology or canonical model lets you validate across systems, and graph features improve matching. One example rule: two product nodes are the same if their names are more than 95% similar and they have identical clustering coefficients.

Database drivers

Drivers are client-side middleware that connect applications over the Bolt protocol or HTTP. Official drivers: Java, JavaScript, Spring/OGM, Python, .NET, Go; community drivers for Ruby, Clojure, PHP, Haskell, Erlang/Elixir, Perl, Py2Neo, and C/C++. Even Neo4j Browser uses a (JavaScript) driver.

driver = GraphDatabase.driver(uri, AuthTokens.basic(user, password));   // 1. one per app
try (Session session = driver.session()) {                              // 2. sessions are cheap
  return session.readTransaction(tx ->                                  // 3. declare read vs write
    tx.run("MATCH (a:Person)-[:FRIEND]->(b:Person) WHERE a.name = $name RETURN b.name",
           parameters("name", name)));                                  // 4. parameterise

  1. Drivers are expensive (connections, authentication): create one for the application's lifetime.
  2. Sessions are cheap: use freely. They support async APIs and bookmarks (causal consistency).
  3. Declare read or write so a cluster can route well.
  4. Parameterise: no re-parsing or re-planning, and protection against injection. Neo4j 5 auto-parameterises where it can.

How Neo4j Graph Data Science works

  1. Read projected graph: choose a sub-graph by labels and types or by Cypher.
  2. Load: into a compact in-memory format.
  3. Execute: run the algorithm in parallel with your parameters.
  4. Store: stream results to the caller, mutate the projection, or write back to the graph.
CALL gds.graph.project.cypher('gds-example-graph',
  'MATCH (p:Person) RETURN id(p) AS id',
  'MATCH (p1:Person)-[:FRIEND]->(p2:Person) RETURN id(p1) AS source, id(p2) AS target, "FRIEND" AS type');
CALL gds.betweenness.write('gds-example-graph', {writeProperty:'betweennessCentrality'});

Projection contract: node query returns id; relationship query returns source, target, type, and the relationships must connect projected nodes. A projection lives in the graph catalogue and can be reused. On the small social graph, Karl has the highest betweenness: without him the network falls apart. Bloom can size nodes by score. There are 65+ algorithms.

Scale up, not out. GDS keeps the projection in one large memory space for locality: a neighbour is a pointer dereference, not a network call. The authors argue scale-up is currently faster and often cheaper than distributed processing, and that CPUs beat GPUs because many graph algorithms aren't linear algebra.

Traditional software takes input and a hand-written function to produce output. ML inverts this: from inputs and past outputs it derives the function (a regression, a neural network, or geometric learning on graphs). Knowledge graphs are excellent fuel, in two ways:

In-graph ML
Predict how the graph will evolve: missing relationships, labels, and properties. The graph enriches itself.
Graph feature engineering
Extract features (PageRank, community, node embeddings) to train a better external model. Graphs give more, and higher-quality, features than columns like age or postcode.

Either way, model and graph form a feedback loop: using the model enriches the graph, which improves the model.

    CALL gds.beta.pipeline.linkPrediction.addNodeProperty('actors-pipeline', 'fastRP',
  {mutateProperty: 'embedding', embeddingDimension: 256, randomSeed: 42})

    CALL gds.beta.pipeline.linkPrediction.addFeature('actors-pipeline', 'cosine',
  {nodeProperties: ['embedding']})
  6. Split into test, train, and feature sets (positive and negative examples are generated for you): 
    CALL gds.beta.pipeline.linkPrediction.configureSplit('actors-pipeline',
  {testFraction: 0.25, trainFraction: 0.6, validationFolds: 3})
  7. Add model candidates (logistic regression, random forest, multilayer perceptron) and auto-tuning: configureAutoTuning('actors-pipeline', {maxTrials: 100}).

You will be able to

Step 1: Data preparation

Harmonise units and types, make nulls real nulls (not the string "null"), unify case and encoding, and trim spaces and special characters. Load each record as a Person node with a source property for provenance, then build comparable matching properties:

LOAD CSV WITH HEADERS FROM "file:///ds1.csv" AS row
CREATE (p:Person) SET p.source = "ds1", p += properties(row);

// Lowest common denominator for birth info: year of birth
MATCH (p:Person) WHERE p.source = "ds1" SET p.m_yob = toInteger(p.yob);
MATCH (p:Person) WHERE p.source = "ds2" SET p.m_yob = date().year - toInteger(p.age);
MATCH (p:Person) WHERE p.source = "ds3" SET p.m_yob = date(p.dob).year;

// Canonical "first last" lower-case name
MATCH (p:Person) WHERE p.source = "ds2"
WITH p, split(p.name, ",") AS parts
SET p.m_fullname = toLower(trim(parts[1]) + ' ' + trim(parts[0]));

Optionally generate blocking keys (postcode, or synthetic ones from algorithms or ML) to avoid n² comparisons. Preparation can also happen beforehand in pandas or SQL; choose for simplicity, robustness, and performance.

Step 2: Entity matching

Three kinds of features: strong identifiers (SSN, passport), weak identifiers (name), and non-identifying features (year of birth).

  1. SAME_AS for a strong-identifier match. 
    MATCH (p1:Person), (p2:Person)
WHERE p1.source <> p2.source AND id(p1) > id(p2)      // symmetric: compare once
  AND (p1.ssn = p2.ssn OR p1.passport_no = p2.passport_no)
CREATE (p1)-[:SAME_AS {ssn_match: p1.ssn = p2.ssn,
                       passport_match: p1.passport_no = p2.passport_no}]->(p2)
  2. Weighted SIMILAR for a weak-identifier match above a threshold, where no SAME_AS exists. 
    MATCH (p1:Person), (p2:Person)
WHERE NOT (p1)-[:SAME_AS]-(p2) AND p1.source <> p2.source AND id(p1) > id(p2)
  AND apoc.text.jaroWinklerDistance(p1.m_fullname, p2.m_fullname) < 0.2
CREATE (p1)-[:SIMILAR {sim_score: 1 - apoc.text.jaroWinklerDistance(p1.m_fullname, p2.m_fullname)}]->(p2)
    Jaro–Winkler is a distance, so 1 − distance gives a 0–1 similarity where higher is stronger.
  3. Delete SIMILAR where strong identifiers conflict (Husein vs Hana Sprull: similar names, different SSNs).
  4. Apply a correcting factor from non-identifying features: ×1.1 if birth years are close, ×0.9 if not.
  5. Delete SIMILAR below a minimum score.

Step 3: Master entities

Each connected group of SAME_AS and SIMILAR nodes is one real entity. Weakly Connected Components (WCC, which ignores direction) finds them.

CALL gds.graph.project('identity-wcc', 'Person', ['SAME_AS','SIMILAR']);

CALL gds.wcc.stream('identity-wcc') YIELD nodeId, componentId
WITH gds.util.asNode(nodeId) AS person, componentId AS golden_id
MERGE (pg:PersonMaster {uid: golden_id})
  ON CREATE SET pg.fullname = person.m_fullname, pg.ssn = person.ssn, pg.passport_no = person.passport_no
  ON MATCH  SET pg.ssn = coalesce(pg.ssn, person.ssn), pg.passport_no = coalesce(pg.passport_no, person.passport_no)
MERGE (pg)-[:HAS_REFERENCE]->(person)

"Sidney Bernardy", "sydney joanne bernardy", and "syd j bernardy" collapse into one PersonMaster with traceable references to each source record. You can return it as JSON for other systems.

Ongoing work. Sources change. With deltas, remove deleted records first, since additions and removals can merge or split components. Graph features (relationship properties, neighbours, degree, centrality) are automatically available as extra matching evidence.

The Amazon–Google catalogue dataset (1,363 Amazon and 3,226 Google products) has no strong identifiers. Tokenise names into Word nodes, then compare products by the words they share:

Fraud detection

US fraud losses were $5.8 billion in 2021 (FTC), with about $1 billion in banking and 70% annual growth; UK banks lost about £700 million. Every $1 lost costs a further $4.23 in handling and reputational damage. Traditional rule-based risk scores and manual review are slow, expensive, and inflexible. The model and a sanity check

Each card is at the centre of its subgraph: purchases made WITH the card and chained by NEXT; a person who is ACCOUNT_HOLDER, LIVES_AT an address, and OWNS a phone. Normal customers form disconnected islands. With 2,399,999 people you'd expect 2,399,999 communities.

Fraud rings vs innocent bystanders

  WHERE p1 <> p2

Dependency knowledge graphs

You will be able to
  • Model dependencies, qualified dependencies, and temporal validity.
  • Propagate impact under aggregate, redundant, and threshold semantics.
  • Validate a dependency graph, and find single points of failure and root causes.

Project plans ("B can't start before A"), software supply chains (is a vulnerable library used directly or transitively?), and company ownership (find the ultimate beneficial owner for anti-money-laundering checks) are all dependency modelling: direct dependencies are relationships that build into networks of transitive dependencies.

Dependencies as a graph

In SQL, "does A depend on F?" needs one self-join per level, and a "no" answer requires exploring every chain. In a graph, it's one variable-length pattern:

LOAD CSV WITH HEADERS FROM "file:///dependencies.csv" AS row
MERGE (a:Element {id: row.element})
MERGE (b:Element {id: row.depends_on})
MERGE (a)-[:DEPENDS_ON]->(b)

MATCH path = (:Element {id:'A'})-[:DEPENDS_ON*]->(:Element {id:'F'})
RETURN path

Qualified and temporal dependencies

Put composable numbers on relationships: OWNS {part: 25%}, CONSUMES {capacity: …}. Add validity start and end properties, and query with a timestamp parameter t to see dependencies active at that time (past or future, as in booking systems).

Multidependency semantics

Additive / aggregate
All dependencies are active at once and each contributes a weight: portfolio assets, bonded network links, virtual drives. 
parent_impact = Σ child_impact × child_weight
A tipping point may exist where the parent fails completely.
Redundant / protection
Only one dependency needs to work; the rest are spares: high-availability servers, alternative broadcast routes, supply routes (Singapore–Felixstowe via Suez, or via the Trans-Siberian Railway). 
parent_impact = min(child impacts)
Protection nodes absorb failures.

Threshold generalisation, e.g. a database cluster needing 2 of 3 primaries for quorum:

parent_impact = 0 if Σ(1 − child_impact) ≥ parent_threshold else 1

Impact propagation in Cypher

Each DEPENDS_ON has mode ('AGG' or 'RED') and a weight abs.

// 1. Scope: everything that could possibly be affected (topology only)
:param declared => ["I","K","J"]
MATCH (e:Element)-[:DEPENDS_ON*]->(impacted)
WHERE impacted.id IN $declared
RETURN collect(DISTINCT e.id) AS max_impact_list     // ["H","Q","P","O","N","G","B","A","F"]

// 2a. Redundant node: impacted only if ALL its alternatives are (binary min)
MATCH (e:Element {id:$current})-[d:DEPENDS_ON {mode:"RED"}]->(dependee)
WITH e.id AS element, CASE WHEN dependee.id IN $declared THEN 1 ELSE 0 END AS partial
RETURN element, min(partial) AS derived_impact

// 2b. Aggregate node: weighted sum of declared severities (0..1)
:param declared => {I: 0.5, K: 1, J: 0.33}
MATCH (e:Element {id:$current})-[d:DEPENDS_ON {mode:"AGG"}]->()
WITH e.id AS element, coalesce($declared[endNode(d).id], 0) * d.abs AS partial
RETURN element, sum(partial) AS derived_impact

Validating the graph

  1. No cycles: a dependency graph is a DAG. MATCH cycle = (e:Element)-[:DEPENDS_ON*]->(e) RETURN cycle. Limit scope (e.id IN $node_subset) or depth (*3..45) on large graphs.
  2. Aggregates add up: relative weights sum to 1 (shareholders total 100%), or absolute weights equal the node's total (five bonded 20 Gbps lines = 100 Gbps).
  3. Consumption doesn't exceed production: compare capacity from dependees (sum of AGG plus min of RED) with use by dependents; report percentage used, which is also useful for capacity planning.
  4. Redundancy meets thresholds: a redundant set needs at least threshold members. One primary server with threshold 2 can never be satisfied.

A failure may reveal a real provisioning problem or a bug in the ingestion pipeline; find out which.

Single points of failure

Three "redundant" database primaries are no help if two run on the same VM. A SPOF is two dependency chains from one element converging on the same node:

MATCH alertPath = (spof)<-[:DEPENDS_ON*]-(e:Element)-[:DEPENDS_ON*]->(spof)
WHERE e.id = $selected_node_id
RETURN alertPath

Some SPOFs can't be removed (a geographic bottleneck in an energy grid), but you should monitor them and plan responses such as backup generators for hospitals.

Root cause analysis

The inverse of impact propagation, used for alarm or event correlation in service assurance. Find leaf nodes (which depend on nothing) that explain several symptoms, then rank them:

  • Precision: share of observed symptoms the candidate explains.
  • Recall: share of the candidate's potential impact that was actually observed.
  • F-score: 2PR/(P+R).
MATCH (e:Element)-[:DEPENDS_ON*0..]->(x)
WHERE e.id IN $symptoms AND NOT (x)-[:DEPENDS_ON]->()
WITH x, collect(DISTINCT e.id) AS explains WHERE size(explains) > 1
MATCH (x)<-[:DEPENDS_ON*0..]-(y)
WITH x, explains, collect(DISTINCT y.id) AS maxImpact
WITH x, toFloat(size(explains))/size($symptoms) AS precision,
     toFloat(size([i IN maxImpact WHERE i IN $symptoms]))/size(maxImpact) AS recall
RETURN x.id, precision, recall, 2*precision*recall/(precision+recall) AS fscore
ORDER BY fscore DESC
Symptoms N, O, P, H, I, A, E. Candidates I, K, J each explain 5 of 7 (precision 0.71), but K and J would have caused many more failures than observed (recall 0.56 and 0.50). I balances both (recall 0.83, F 0.77) and is the most likely root cause. E (precision 0.29, recall 1.0) is a separate, independent cluster. Results are only as good as the symptom list, so combine with time and domain heuristics.

Semantic search and document similarity

You will be able to
  • Annotate documents with entities (NER) and store them as a graph.
  • Compute salience-weighted, explainable document similarity and handle cold start.
  • Link entities to an organizing principle for true semantic search ("things, not strings").

Keyword search struggles with plurals, spelling variants, abbreviations, and acronyms, and ranks by term frequency or TF-IDF. Google improved ranking with PageRank (links between pages) and meaning with the Knowledge Graph ("things, not strings", Amit Singhal). Knowledge graphs resolve synonyms and related concepts, and open the door to question answering (Module 25).

From strings to things: annotation with NER

In "The New York Times… headquarters… in New York City," NER separates the organisation from the location even though they share words.

from transformers import pipeline
ner_pipe = pipeline("ner", aggregation_strategy="simple")    # default model: bert-base-NER
for entity in ner_pipe("Twitter chair Patrick Pichette joins graph data platform Neo4j board of directors."):
    print(entity)
# {'entity_group': 'ORG', 'score': 0.996, 'word': 'Twitter', ...}
# {'entity_group': 'PER', 'score': 0.997, 'word': 'Patrick Pichette', ...}
# {'entity_group': 'ORG', 'score': 0.831, 'word': 'Neo4j', ...}

NER returns a type (bert-base-NER: LOC, ORG, PER, MISC) and a salience, how central the entity is to the text. Use a domain-trained model for technical text. Store the results:

MERGE (a:Article {url:$url}) ON CREATE SET a.title = $title, a.text = $frg
MERGE (p:Person {name:$author}) MERGE (a)-[:has_author]->(p)
WITH a UNWIND $entityList AS entity
MERGE (e:Entity {name: entity.word, type: entity.type})
MERGE (a)-[:references {salience: entity.score}]->(e)

Now you can ask "what is this article about?" and "which articles mention this entity?" with the same pattern, and track entity trends month by month, which is far more meaningful than word counts.

Making annotation semantic

Plain NER has two gaps: entities aren't disambiguated (UK vs United Kingdom), and there are no relationships between entities (Wales PART_OF the United Kingdom). The fix is named-entity linking (disambiguation) to an organizing principle such as MeSH or the Disease Ontology in life sciences, or Wikidata.

  1. Extract entities with a service that returns identifiers. The GCP Natural Language API adds wikipedia_url metadata, e.g. Patrick Pichette, salience 0.63.
  2. Use a cross-referenced knowledge base: Wikidata states <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo4j> schema:about wd:Q1628290, and Neo4j is an instance of graph database (wd:Q595971).
// Load the organizing principle (SKOS/OWL/RDFS) with neosemantics

  YIELD node, value SET node.processed = true

 {batchMode:"BATCH_SINGLE", batchSize:10, params:{key:$key}})

Several organizing principles give several complementary paths. Without built-in linking, annotate concepts with alternative labels or include/exclude regexes (COVID-19: covid.*, corona.*, but not sars.*) and write selection logic using context, distance, or centrality.

Case study: NASA Lessons Learned. Decades of lessons since Apollo were siloed; the old system held under 1% of 20 million documents and keyword searches returned around 1,100 documents to read. A knowledge graph linking lessons by extracted entities and topics gives faster, fewer, more relevant results and shows topic correlations. One engineer: "This has saved us at least a year and over $2M in research and development towards our Mission to Mars planning."

Natural language generation

Nouns become nodes and verbs become relationships ("Dan loves Ann"), so reversing that gives proto-sentences:

MATCH (x)-[r]->(y)
RETURN x.name + " " + toLower(replace(type(r), "_", " ")) + " " + y.name AS sentence
// "Euroyacht Limited officer of TUC LIMITED"   (Panama Papers sample)

MATCH (n:Entity) UNWIND keys(n) AS property
RETURN n.name + "'s " + replace(property, "_", " ") + " is " + n[property] AS sentence
// "TUC LIMITED's jurisdiction is MLT"

Limits: it assumes a name property, cryptic names (sub_109) read badly, and incoming relationships need inverse phrasing. The general fix is to annotate the ontology (a "talkable" vocabulary):

mv:Movie  talk:name "title" .     mv:Person talk:name "name" .
mv:ACTED_IN rdfs:domain mv:Person ; rdfs:range mv:Movie ;
  talk:direct  "acted in"@default , "is in the cast of"@long , "worked in"@short ;
  talk:inverse "has $o in it"@default , "includes"@short .
mv:released talk:direct "was released in"@default , "The release year of $s was"@long .

Load it with n10s.onto.import.fetch (or plain Cypher). A generic query then picks the naming property, chooses direct or inverse phrasing by relationship direction, and substitutes $s and $o: "Hugo Weaving worked in Cloud Atlas, V for Vendetta, The Matrix…". Richer generation can use WordNet or ML. Bot frameworks such as Rasa can query Neo4j through knowledge-base actions.

Lexical databases: WordNet as a graph

WordNet groups words into synsets linked by semantic relations. Import its RDF release with neosemantics:

CREATE CONSTRAINT n10s_unique_uri ON (r:Resource) ASSERT r.uri IS UNIQUE;
CALL n10s.graphconfig.init({handleVocabUris: "IGNORE"});
CALL n10s.rdf.import.fetch(".../english-wordnet-2021.ttl.gz", "Turtle");

// Meanings of "clear": Form ← LexicalEntry → LexicalSense → LexicalConcept
MATCH (lemma:Form)<-[:canonicalForm]-(le:LexicalEntry)-[:sense]->()-[:isLexicalizedSenseOf]->(concept)
WHERE lemma.writtenRep = "clear"
RETURN le.partOfSpeech, concept.definition

Count senses to find the most polysemous word ("break", 75 senses), or traverse in reverse to find all words for one concept. Hypernym (more general) and hyponym (more specific) relations form a taxonomy (a globe is a kind of sphere); there are also meronyms (part-of) and more.

From knowledge graphs to knowledge lakes, and putting it all together

You will be able to
  • Describe how knowledge graph deployments evolve and what a knowledge lake is.
  • Reconcile the research view (Part I) with the practitioner view (Part II).
  • Plan a first knowledge graph project.

How deployments grow

  1. Departmental knowledge graph fed by one department's data.
  2. Cross-functional graphs interlocked through ontologies and middleware (compliance, customer 360).
  3. Foundational layer for enterprise-wide reuse, with other systems built on its curated data.

Success creates a risk: many knowledge graphs becoming ungovernable, "knowledge graphs all the way down."

Knowledge lakes

A knowledge lake is an architecture and usage pattern that is itself a knowledge graph and can subsume other knowledge graphs and non-graph data. It sits next to data warehouses and data lakes, holding a curated subset of the organisation's data as a graph for many projects: "a collection of knowledge graphs designed for reuse." It doesn't replace the data lake, which is built for volume and throughput with meaning deferred. The knowledge lake adds meaning afterwards and becomes the main lens for consuming business-meaningful data. It can start as small as one system and grow, with governance and automation to keep it fresh. "We are drowning in information but starved for knowledge" (John Naisbitt).

Both sources agree that a knowledge graph is more than a graph: it needs an explicit layer of meaning (schema, ontology, organizing principle), a way to manage identity, and techniques, deductive and inductive, to grow and refine knowledge. Both favour incremental, "pay-as-you-go" or agile construction over big upfront modelling, and both note that enterprise ontologies tend to be lightweight taxonomies.

A first-project checklist

  1. Pick one use case with a clear consumer. Write competency questions it must answer.
  2. Choose the lightest organizing principle that answers them: property graph, then taxonomy, then ontology. Reuse standards (Schema.org, SKOS, FIBO, SNOMED CT) where they fit.
  3. Design identity: keys and node-key constraints, persistent identifiers, an entity-resolution plan.
  4. Load a representative slice first (LOAD CSV), check the model, then bulk load.
  5. Decide what to materialise and what to virtualise (ETL vs query rewriting / APOC virtual resources).
  6. Validate with constraints or shapes; measure quality (accuracy, completeness, consistency, understandability).
  7. Enrich with algorithms (centrality, communities, similarity) and ML (link prediction), writing results back.
  8. Record provenance and context; plan licensing and privacy before publishing.
  9. Keep it current and encourage use: each use enriches the graph.
