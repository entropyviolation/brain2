# Quality and authenticity, 5 October 2026

What “good” means for every scent and material these plans actually use, and how a cheap listing fails. Prices and which house to order from are in [shopping-list.md](shopping-list.md) and [price-research-2026-10-05.md](price-research-2026-10-05.md). This note does not change a formula.

## 1. Method note

The material list is the shopping list, the pantry lines in [plan.md](plan.md), and the chemicals the formula files and laundry notes tell a person to buy or to keep as a labeled test: borax, distilled white vinegar, and sodium percarbonate. Unbought materials (cade, raw oakmoss, sweet almond, Galaxolide, rice ferment, propylene glycol) appear only as rejects.

“High quality” here means the identity the formula was written for. A rarer grade that smells different is not an upgrade. Organic certification, an ISO 9001 factory badge, and the words “therapeutic grade” or “100% pure” are not assays. Organic and geographic marks track farming and custody. They do not detect synthetic linalool poured in after the still, a phthalate cut, or the wrong salt.

Documents that count are lot-matched: the lot on the bottle is the lot on the paper, the Latin name or INCI matches the row below, and a dilution states its percent and its solvent. A gas chromatogram with no lot number, or a certificate reused across many brands, does not count. An IFRA certificate states a use limit. It is not a purity assay. A certificate of analysis (COA) states what is in the lot. Fragrance materials need both. Marketplace oils and aroma chemicals stay off this cart, including listings that attach a COA. That refusal is already in the price note. The evidence for it is below.

Home checks in the receiving list are label, lot match, look, and smell. They are not a laboratory.

## 3. Per material

### Lavender Maillette

ISO 3515:2002 is the oil of *Lavandula angustifolia*, population and clonal. Maillette is a French clone of that species. Lavandin (*Lavandula × intermedia*) is a different plant and a harsher, camphor-heavy oil. Spike lavender is different again.

Pokajewicz and colleagues (Industrial Crops and Products, 2024) tested commercial oils labeled *L. angustifolia*. Of 51 such samples, 51% were genuine, 6% were lavandin, 14% were fortified with synthetics, and 29% were both lavandin and synthetics. The synthetics they name are linalool, linalyl acetate, 3,5,5-trimethylhexyl acetate, α-terpinyl acetate, and dipropylene glycol. They found ISO 3515 strict enough to fail genuine oils, and the European Pharmacopoeia monograph better at failing the fakes. Ask for a lot chromatogram, not a bare ISO logo.

NOW Foods, using GC-MS/FID on 28 Amazon lavender oils, reported that 3 met ISO criteria for authentic *L. angustifolia* with no adulteration. Failures included the wrong species or a blend, carrier or mineral-oil dilution, and synthetic fragrance materials. Two bottles contained diethyl phthalate at 5.76% and 16.0%. NutraIngredients reported the same round on 22 May 2026. That is why a marketplace COA does not reopen Amazon, eBay, AliExpress, or Alibaba.

A bulk liter oxidizes and is more lavender than this pantry uses. Buy the small Maillette bottle.

### Virginia cedarwood and Atlas cedarwood

ISO 4724:2004 is Virginian cedarwood, steam-distilled wood of *Juniperus virginiana*. Its profile is cedrene, thujopsene, and cedrol. Atlas cedar is *Cedrus atlantica*. Its wood oil is dominated by himachalenes and atlantones and does not smell like pencil shavings. Chinese cedarwood (*Cupressus funebris*) is the cheap oil most often hiding under the single trade name “cedarwood.” Texas cedar (*Juniperus ashei*) is closer to Virginia than to Atlas and is still the wrong bottle when the label does not say so.

The solid perfume and the clean-woods accord use Virginia on purpose. Atlas is the balsamic cedar. One does not replace the other. Neither is a case for a four-ounce drum.

### Balsam fir, black spruce, cypress, Scotch pine

These four are species oils. Balsam fir is *Abies balsamea*. Black spruce is *Picea mariana*, not Norway spruce. Cypress on this cart is Mediterranean *Cupressus sempervirens*, not blue cypress and not juniper. Scotch pine is *Pinus sylvestris* needle, not an unnamed pine.

They are less often rebuilt with phthalates than lavender, rose, and sandalwood. The ordinary fraud is the wrong tree. A lot GC that names the species is the document. A fresh, cool bottle matters more than an organic seal: oxidized needle oils go sharp. A four-ounce mixed set from an unnamed wholesaler is the wrong size and the wrong paperwork.

### Hinoki wood

The cart oil is *Chamaecyparis obtusa* wood, plantation sawmill, not the leaf. Leaf oil is a sharper monoterpene oil. A “hinoki fragrance oil” is a compound. The document is species, plant part, and a lot GC from a seller who will put a lot number on both. Storage is ordinary for a wood oil: full, dark, cool. A cheaper East Asian listing with no lot GC is not a saving.

### Ylang ylang complete

ISO 3063:2004 covers *Cananga odorata* forma genuina from Madagascar, Mayotte, and the Comoros, and it says the oil is not generally sold as a whole. Trade collects Extra super, Extra, First, Second, and Third. “Complete” means the whole distillation, light fractions and heavy fractions together. Extra is a different, costlier smell. Ylang II is a later fraction. Cananga (forma *macrophylla*) is a different oil.

Accept complete, with a GC that shows both ends of the distillation. Reject a fraction. Organic does not choose the fraction.

### Rose geranium

ISO 4731:2012 is geranium oil from *Pelargonium* × spp., the plants sold as *Pelargonium graveolens*, and it separates origin types. Bourbon, Egyptian, and Chinese oils are all geranium and do not smell the same. The frauds are citronella (*Cymbopogon*), added geraniol, and a rebuilt oil. This bottle is the soap and laundry rose, not a substitute for Turkish rose. A lot GC from a named distillate seller is the document. A harsh citronella smell is a reject.

### Patchouli, dark

ISO 3757:2002 is *Pogostemon cablin*. Dark patchouli is an aged grade of that oil, not a second species. Cuts use gurjun balsam, cedar, vegetable oil, and synthetic patchoulol. A pale clear oil can be genuine and still be the wrong grade for this cart. Accept dark *P. cablin* with a GC. A one-ounce bulk bottle from a new house does not beat one lot-matched 15 ml.

### Frankincense CO2, India

This bottle is a supercritical extract of *Boswellia serrata*, India. *Boswellia sacra* and the oils sold as *carterii* are pinene-led steam oils. *Boswellia papyrifera* is rich in octyl acetate. The American Botanical Council’s laboratory guidance on *B. serrata* oleogum resins treats undisclosed substitution with other *Boswellia* species as the adulteration pattern.

Pawan Kumar Ojha, Darbin Kumar Poudel, and colleagues (Plants, 2022) found commercial oils sold as *B. carteri* adulterated with synthetic limonene, synthetic octyl acetate, castor oil, phthalate, *B. occulta*, and *B. serrata*. Species substitution runs in both directions. Accept a CO2 labeled *B. serrata*, India, with a GC. Reject a Somali or Omani steam oil as this bottle. A sweeter carterii is a later purchase, not a cheaper serrata.

### Sweet orange, pink grapefruit, bitter orange peel

ISO 3140 is expressed sweet orange (*Citrus sinensis*). ISO 3053 is expressed grapefruit (*Citrus × paradisi*). The 1 ml bitter sample is cold-pressed *Citrus aurantium* peel, which is phototoxic and is not petitgrain.

Expressed citrus is mostly limonene. Oxidized limonene is a sensitizer, so a tired lot is a bad lot even when the species was right. IFRA’s furocoumarin policy caps the marker 5-MOP (bergapten) at 15 ppm in sun-exposed leave-on products, and the same guidance points to a tighter 1 ppm figure for sun-protection products from the older SCCNFP opinion, as summarized by the Tisserand Institute. Grapefruit and expressed bitter orange stay inside the night-and-soap limits already written in the formulas. Freshness is the quality spend: a cheap liter that sits open is the false economy. Folded and terpeneless oils are different products.

### Lemon, distilled, and bergamot FCF

ISO 855 is lemon oil obtained by expression. ISO 3520:2022 is Calabrian expressed bergamot, and its own text points to ISO 7358, the HPLC method for bergapten in bergamot, lemon, bitter orange, and lime that have been fully or partly reduced in bergapten. Expressed bergamot in that standard is the phototoxic oil. FCF means furanocoumarin-free (bergapten removed, typically by fractional distillation). Distilled lemon is the matching idea for lemon.

The European Medicines Agency herbal report on bergamot oil describes FCF as the oil with phototoxic constituents removed. The Tisserand Institute states the same for bergamot FCF and for distilled lemon. Accept the word distilled on lemon, the word FCF on bergamot, and a bergapten number. Reject expressed lemon, expressed bergamot, and any “ISO 3520 bergamot” offered as the daylight oil. Organic FCF is still FCF first. Dropping the process to save the organic line is the wrong saving.

### Ginger CO2

Steam-distilled ginger and CO2 ginger are both *Zingiber officinale* and do not contain the same weight of gingerols. The CO2 is the warmer, heavier material these accords use. Accept species plus the process. Reject steam oil and galangal. Five milliliters is the right size.

### Amyris

*Amyris balsamifera* is West Indian sandalwood only as a trade nickname. It is not *Santalum*. Elemol and related alcohols mark it. Accept the species. Reject a sandalwood label, and reject an amyris label on a bottle of cedar.

### Peppermint

ISO 856:2006 is *Mentha × piperita* from the origins that standard groups (United States, United Kingdom, France, Italy, India, China). Cornmint is *Mentha arvensis*, a cheaper, harsher mint, often partly dementholized. Spearmint is carvone and smells different. Accept piperita with a GC. Reject arvensis. A second “mint” bottle is not a quality upgrade.

### Clove bud

ISO 3142:1997 is clove bud oil, *Syzygium aromaticum*. Leaf and stem oils are cheaper and poorer in eugenyl acetate. Accept the word bud and a GC. Reject leaf oil. Clove is a sensitizer at leave-on doses the formulas already cap; a stronger leaf oil is not a bargain.

### Nutmeg

ISO 3215:1998 is Indonesian-type nutmeg oil, *Myristica fragrans*. The cart does not require the Indonesian type, and it does require nutmeg rather than mace. Safrole in nutmeg oil sits under IFRA rules, so the GC is the identity check. Accept *M. fragrans* seed oil. Reject a random “spice oil.”

### Black pepper and pink pepper

Black pepper is *Piper nigrum* steam oil. An oleoresin is pungent and is a different ingredient. Pink pepper on this cart is *Schinus molle*, not *Schinus terebinthifolia* and not *Piper nigrum*. Accept the species named in the shopping list. The allergy note on pink pepper is a safety rule, not a purity test.

### Petitgrain bigarade

Bigarade means bitter-orange leaf and twig, *Citrus aurantium*. It is the daylight orange-flower note. ISO 3064:2000 is petitgrain, Paraguayan type, a trade grade of bitter-orange petitgrain, not sweet-orange leaf and not the peel. Accept leaf or twig of bitter orange. Reject sweet-orange petitgrain and the cold-pressed peel, which is the separate 1 ml sample.

### Labdanum absolute, 10%

The cart material is a solvent extract of *Cistus ladanifer* resin, already at 10%. Steam-distilled cistus leaf oil is pinene-led and is not this smell. Raw gum does not pour. Accept species, “absolute,” the 10% figure, and the name of the solvent, plus a residual-solvent line. Reject a neat ounce, a second cistus oil, and a dilution whose solvent is a secret. Glass droppers belong with this bottle because the absolute glues plastic. That is handling, not a second purchase.

### Benzoin absolute, Sumatra

Sumatra benzoin is *Styrax benzoin*. Siam benzoin is *Styrax tonkinensis* and smells cleaner and more vanilla, because the acid profile differs. Raw tears are for incense and will not dissolve in a roller. Accept Sumatra absolute, residual solvent named. Reject Siam as a silent swap, tears, and a second bottle already dissolved in propylene glycol. Benzoin is not a preservative.

### Jasmine sambac, 10%

*Jasminum sambac* absolute is the flower these trials name. *Jasminum grandiflorum* is heavier and is not on the cart. Neat sambac is a tar. This bottle is 10% in fractionated coconut, which clouds in alcohol; the accords already keep a heavy jasmine dose in jojoba for that reason. Accept sambac, 10%, carrier named. Jasmine absolute is a classic target for synthetic benzyl acetate and indole. A lot GC from a perfumery or natural-isolate house is the document. Reject a fragrance oil and a “jasmine essential oil,” which is not how this flower is produced in ordinary trade.

### Turkish rose by-absolute and rose hydrosol

*Rosa damascena* absolute and rose otto are different products. Otto is the steam distillate. Absolute is a solvent extract and carries phenylethyl alcohol the otto lacks. Geranium is the soap rose and does not replace either. “By-absolute” has to be defined by the seller’s paper: species, Turkey, process, and a GC. Synthetic phenylethyl alcohol and geranium cuts are the frauds. A 1 ml lot-matched bottle is the quality buy. A 5 ml “pure absolute” from an unnamed exporter is the risk.

Rose hydrosol is the distillation water, for the face and an optional serum. It is not a fragrance oil shaken into water. Ask what keeps it from growing. A gallon of unpreserved hydrosol is a microbial bargain, not a rose bargain. Reject any water that lists fragrance or parfum as the rose.

### Vanilla CO2 and vanillin

Vanilla CO2 is a supercritical extract of *Vanilla planifolia*, dark and thick, with natural vanillin plus the other bean phenolics. Synthetic vanillin is a different, legitimate bottle for the shampoo bar and the dryer. Ethyl vanillin is stronger and is not either bottle. Isotope methods (SNIF-NMR and stable carbon ratios) are how a lab catches synthetic vanillin in a natural extract. For 1 ml, the realistic document is species, process, and a house that sells both the CO2 and the crystal as different SKUs. Reject a clear, cheap “vanilla CO2,” and reject a vanilla absolute as a surprise upgrade. Coumarin next to tonka is a second sweet the cart already refused.

Tonka absolute is *Dipteryx odorata*, coumarin-rich, a perfume material and not a flavor. The same isotope logic applies to synthetic coumarin. Accept the absolute with the solvent named. IFRA restricts coumarin in finished products; that is a dose rule in the formula, not a reason to buy a mystery solid.

### Bitter almond, FFPA

Crude bitter-almond oil is distilled after amygdalin in the kernel breaks down to benzaldehyde and hydrogen cyanide (prussic acid). Perfumer & Flavorist (November/December 1991) describes the rectified oil as FFPA: a sensitive ferrous-salt test is used to show the acid is gone, and the oil is the benzaldehyde fraction that remains. The European Pharmacopoeia almond-oil monographs are the fatty oil (the carrier), not this essential oil. IFRA restricts benzaldehyde, and bitter almond oil sits in that standard as a natural source of it.

Accept only a lot that says prussic acid removed or FFPA. Reject unrectified oil, a bottle that is only benzaldehyde, and sweet-almond fatty oil. FFPA oil is still not for eating. Cherry fragrance oil remains the candy note.

### Vetiver, Java

ISO 4716:2013 covers *Chrysopogon zizanioides* from Réunion, China, Haiti, Indonesia, Madagascar, Brazil, and the Comoros. Those origins do not smell alike. Java means Indonesia: smoky and earthy. Haiti is cleaner. Accept origin plus a GC. Vetiver is viscous and expensive, so mineral oil is the classic cut. A 15 ml “for soap” from a new wholesaler is not a quality upgrade on the 5 ml Java bottle.

### Fossilized amber, 5%

Geological amber is not a steam-distilled essential oil in ordinary trade. Perfumery amber is usually labdanum, benzoin, and vanillin, or a house specialty. This cart already has labdanum and benzoin, and it keeps the 5% amber as a separate smoky material. Accept a seller who states the 5% and the solvent and does not call the neat material an essential oil. Reject neat fossilized amber at a price that only makes sense for a compound, and reject using labdanum twice under two names.

### Fractionated coconut, jojoba golden, grapeseed, shea, kokum

Fractionated coconut oil is caprylic/capric triglyceride. It stays liquid and nearly odorless. Whole coconut oil is solid at cool room temperature and smells of coconut. Food “MCT” that includes longer chains can smell and feel different. Accept the INCI and a low acid value. Organic is optional.

Jojoba is a liquid wax of *Simmondsia chinensis*, mostly wax esters, not a triglyceride. Miwa’s wax-ester work, as reviewed by Haidy A. Gad and colleagues (Polymers, 2021), is the chemical reason a synthetic or stand-in wax does not match a random ester pattern. Golden is the unrefined oil. Clear refined is decolorized and is the grade this cart refused. Mineral oil is the dilution. Accept golden, unrefined, from a cosmetic or specialty oil seller with a COA. Reject water-white jojoba as this bottle.

Grapeseed is refined *Vitis vinifera* seed oil, the linoleic oil in the shampoo bar, the solid perfume, and the shimmer spray. Codex Alimentarius CXS 210 is the identity standard for named vegetable oils, including grape seed. The quality that matters in the jar is a low peroxide value and a recent pack date. A paint or cardboard smell means the oil is already oxidized. A commodity refined oil from a cosmetic supplier is enough. A gallon oxidizes before four batches need it.

Refined shea is deodorized *Butyrospermum parkii*. Unrefined shea can be excellent and still smell smoky, which this pantry set aside. Kokum is *Garcinia indica*, the brittle butter. Cocoa does not stand in for it. Accept refined, low odor, with a peroxide value. Paying for a rare nilotica grade is not what these formulas ask.

### Argan

Cosmetic argan is *Argania spinosa* pressed from unroasted kernels. Culinary argan is often roasted and smells of toasted nuts. The Moroccan standard NM 08.5.090 is the national spec those papers cite. Frontiers in Nutrition (2021) summarizes the acidity grades (extra-virgin under 0.8 g/100 g, then fine, ordinary, and lampante) and the campesterol marker: campesterol is only about 0.4% of argan sterols, so sunflower, soy, rapeseed, and olive show up as extra campesterol. Hilali, Charrouf, Soulhi, Hachimi, and Guillaume (Journal of the American Oil Chemists’ Society, 2007) showed that campesterol GC can show adulteration down to about 2%. Said Gharby, Hicham Harhar, Dominique Guillaume, Zoubida Charrouf, and colleagues (Journal of Cosmetic Science, 2014) report the tighter peroxide and ultraviolet limits cosmetic labs were using against the looser Moroccan norm, and they found that cosmetic argan kept at 25°C out of the sun still met that norm at 12 months, while industrial standards were better met inside about 6 months.

For 30 ml from a named oil house, the realistic papers are species, unroasted or cosmetic, acidity, and peroxide value. A bulk drum is where the sterol test earns its cost, and that test is a lab test, not a kitchen test. Organic and the Moroccan PGI speak to origin. They do not replace campesterol if the price is far below a real oil. Reject a roasted kitchen oil for the hair mist, and reject any lot that already smells rancid. This bottle is five mists. A larger jug is how a good oil becomes a stale one.

### Palmarosa

ISO 4727:2021 is *Cymbopogon martinii* var. *motia*. The preview of that standard gives relative density 0.880–0.894, refractive index 1.471–1.478, and optical rotation from −1° to +3°, and it sets a chromatographic profile in which geraniol is the major alcohol and geranyl acetate falls between 5.0% and 13.0%. Var. *sofia* is gingergrass, a different oil. Synthetic geraniol and citronella fractions are the cuts. A bottle that is nearly pure geraniol, with no geranyl acetate, is a rebuild. Accept *motia* with a GC. Organic does not identify the variety.

### Cardamom

ISO 4733:2004 is the steam-distilled fruit oil of *Elettaria cardamomum*. Black cardamom (*Amomum*) is a different spice. Added 1,8-cineole, the eucalyptus move, is the cut to watch on a GC. One milliliter is the right size. A cheap ounce is not a better cardamom.

### Australian sandalwood

ISO 3518:2025 is *Santalum album* heartwood: (Z)-α-santalol 41–55%, (Z)-β-santalol 16–24%, (E,E)-farnesol not detected above 1%. ISO 22769:2022 is Australian *Santalum spicatum*: (Z)-α-santalol 15–30%, (Z)-β-santalol 4.5–20%, (E,E)-farnesol 2.5–18%, (Z)-nuciferol 2–15%, plus bisabolol, bergamotol, and lanceol ranges in that standard. Album is not a stronger spicatum. Amyris is not either species. Synthetic santalols are different molecules.

This cart’s 1 ml is premium *S. spicatum*. Accept a GC that fits ISO 22769. Reject album, amyris, and a “sandalwood” with no species. The expensive album bottle can wait. A cheap album is the one that fails this table.

### Iso E Super, Hedione, ethylene brassylate, Norlimbanol, Cashmeran

These are named aroma chemicals. Iso E Super is an IFF OTNE quality. Hedione is Firmenich methyl dihydrojasmonate; Hedione HC is the high-cis grade, stronger, and not a drop-in. Ethylene brassylate is the musk. It is not Galaxolide. Norlimbanol and Cashmeran are single materials, not “woody amber” or “cashmere” blends. The formulas dilute Norlimbanol to 10% on the bench. The bottle must say whether that dilution was already done, and in what solvent.

The failure mode is a cut: diethyl phthalate, dipropylene glycol, benzyl benzoate, or a different isomer ratio, plus a PDF that is not the lot in the drum. Ask a perfumery reseller of the manufacturer’s material for the IFRA certificate (51st Amendment, 2023, or newer if one has superseded it), the SDS, and a statement of neat strength. An Alibaba COA does not meet that bar. Small bottles match the doses. A two-ounce drum is how these end up in wash water, which the accords already forbid for Iso E Super.

### Veramoss

Veramoss (IFF) and Evernyl are methyl 2,4-dihydroxy-3,6-dimethylbenzoate, CAS 4707-47-5. IFRA’s oakmoss standard limits oakmoss extracts and requires atranol and chloroatranol each below 100 ppm in the extract, with dehydroabietic acid not above 0.1%, as stated in the 51st Amendment materials cited by suppliers and by ScenTree. Veramoss does not contain those lichen allergens. Raw oakmoss is not a more authentic version of this purchase. The only natural oakmoss worth a later look is an IFRA-compliant treated extract inside its own limit, and it is not on this cart.

### Birch tar, rectified, 10%

IFRA prohibits crude birch-wood pyrolysate as a fragrance ingredient. Rectified distillates are allowed only under the PAH specification: benzopyrene and 1,2-benzanthracene are the markers, and together they are not to exceed 1 ppb in the finished product. Cade is a different tar and stays unbought. Accept rectified tar, already at 10% in benzyl benzoate, with the PAH line. Reject neat tar, crude tar, and any “rectified, so it is safe to handle by the spoon” claim. Weigh the 10% bottle. Do not dose it in drops of neat tar.

### Guaiacwood

*Bulnesia sarmientoi* wood oil is the milky wood next to the birch tar. It is not a tar and not guaiac resin. Accept the species from a perfumery house. Reject a campfire compound.

### Fragrance oils and bases

Honeysuckle, cherry, coconut II, and green tea are compounded fragrance oils. Honey lactone essence is a base. There is no ordinary honeysuckle essential oil. Cherry fragrance is the candy; FFPA bitter almond is the pit. Coconut II is a smell; fractionated coconut is odorless. The papers are an IFRA certificate for the current amendment, an allergen declaration, an SDS, and a phthalate statement. A perfumery house can issue those. A craft-marketplace “phthalate-free” JPEG cannot. These oils are stable when sealed. The risk is the solvent, not a short shelf life.

### Vanillin, ethyl maltol, Base de Parfum

Vanillin crystals and ethyl maltol crystals are single molecules. FCC is the food-chemical grade and is the right purity language. Ethyl vanillin and caramel fragrance oil are different materials. Base de Parfum is high-proof perfumer’s alcohol with a denaturant, bought and shipped inside the country. Isopropyl rubbing alcohol and vodka are the wrong solvents. Ask which denaturant it is. Do not import ethanol to save a few dollars.

### SCI, SLSa, CAPB, sodium coco sulfate, caprylyl/capryl glucoside

SCI is sodium cocoyl isethionate. The bar needs the fine powder. Noodles and flakes are the same family and the wrong physical grade. A powder that is mostly free fatty acid will not lather at 40 g in a 100 g bar. The COA needs INCI and percent active matter.

SLSa is sodium lauryl sulfoacetate. Sodium lauryl sulfate and sodium laureth sulfate are different salts with different irritation and dioxane stories. The names are close enough to be a listing trick. Accept the sulfoacetate spelled out, with percent active.

Cocamidopropyl betaine is a liquid. CIR’s 2012 amended report (Burnett and colleagues, International Journal of Toxicology) says the ingredient is safe when formulated to be non-sensitizing, because the impurities DMAPA and amidoamine are the sensitizers. A supplier survey cited there found amidoamine often around 0.5%, with 1.5% discussed as a suggested maximum, and DMAPA often below 100 ppm. Accept a cosmetic liquid with percent active and an impurity statement. A fishy smell means the lot is a poor one. The formula’s 11 g assumes the ordinary liquid, not a powder.

Sodium coco sulfate is its own powder. It is not SCI and not SLS. Dryness matters: a caked jar has taken up water and the gram no longer matches the formula. Caprylyl/capryl glucoside is that INCI, usually sold as an aqueous solution near 60% active, so the COA must state percent active. Coco-glucoside is a longer-chain glucoside and the wrong INCI. These surfactants are commodity chemicals. The money goes to the right INCI and a COA, not to a luxury brand.

### Hydrolyzed rice protein, rice flour, rice starch

The shampoo bar uses two rice materials. The liquid is hydrolyzed rice protein, a preserved cosmetic hydrolysate. The powder is superfine white rice flour or rice starch (*Oryza sativa* starch). Brown rice flour carries oil and color and goes stale. Sports rice-protein powder is a food protein, not this liquid. Rice ferment filtrate is a different INCI and is not in these formulas. Accept the liquid with percent solids and its preservative named, and a white superfine flour or a starch. The water in the liquid protein is why the bar has PE 9010.

### Vitamin E MT-50

T-50 is mixed tocopherols at about 50% in a carrier oil (often soy or sunflower). Gamma- and delta-tocopherols are the antioxidant function in oils. Tocopheryl acetate is a stable ester and a poor antioxidant until it is split. The serum’s Q-MAX already contains some acetate for a different reason. Accept mixed tocopherols with the carrier named. Reject the acetate as the pantry antioxidant. A large jar oxidizes. The dose across four batches is under 2 g.

### PE 9010 and polysorbate 20

PE 9010 is phenoxyethanol plus ethylhexylglycerin. These formulas use 0.5–1% and stay at or under 1.1%. Liquid Germall Plus is a formaldehyde donor and stays off the cart. Phenoxyethanol alone is not the blend. Accept the two INCI names on the COA.

Polysorbate 20 is the water solubilizer. Polysorbate 80 is a different HLB. USP polysorbate 20 limits ethylene oxide to not more than 1 ppm and 1,4-dioxane to not more than 10 ppm. CIR’s 2015 polysorbate report says the industry should keep those ethoxylation impurities low. Old polysorbate goes dark and sharp because the chains oxidize. Accept a fresh polysorbate 20 with a dioxane line. A diner-sized jug is how a good emulsifier becomes a peroxide problem. The glitter mist uses 0.3 g.

### LC995, alkyl benzoate, dimethicone 350

LC995, as the hair-mist formula already specifies, is at least 96% cyclopentasiloxane (D5), D4 at or under 0.099%, and up to 4% D6. Commission Regulation (EU) 2018/35 restricts D4 and D5 to below 0.1% in wash-off cosmetics because of their environmental profile. The hair mist is leave-on. The grade question is still the D4 number, because a generic “cyclomethicone” can be a D4/D5/D6 blend. Accept the COA split. Reject an unspecified cyclomethicone. Dimethicone 350 will not evaporate and will not make this mist. The only dimethicone light enough to stand in, per the hair-mist note, is about 1.5 centistokes, and it is not the serum’s 350.

C12-15 alkyl benzoate is a light ester. Mineral oil, isopropyl myristate, and isoamyl laurate are different slips. Accept the INCI and a low acid value. Dimethicone 350 means 350 centistokes on the COA. Flash point and viscosity are on that sheet so a volatile silicone is not shipped in its place.

### Aristoflex AVC, Q-MAX, propanediol, ceramides

Aristoflex AVC is ammonium acryloyldimethyltaurate/VP copolymer. Carbomer does not tolerate the serum’s salts the same way. Accept the INCI. Q-MAX, in the serum file, is 7.5% ubiquinone and 10% tocopheryl acetate in C12-15 alkyl benzoate. Dry CoQ10 is a different material and is off the cart. The yellow color is the quinone. Keep the jar dark.

Propanediol on an INCI list is 1,3-propanediol. Propylene glycol is 1,2-propanediol. They are not interchangeable in this formula. Accept the isomer on the COA.

The ceramide complex is a premix, and the serum file says it is already preserved with phenoxyethanol and ethylhexylglycerin. Accept a full INCI. Reject a vegetable oil with a ceramide claim.

### Panthenol, hyaluronic acid, niacinamide, allantoin, N-acetyl glucosamine

The serum line is panthenol powder. The cart names dl-panthenol, the racemate. A 75% liquid changes the water math. Calcium pantothenate is a different salt. Accept powder and an assay.

ELMW hyaluronic acid is fermentation-derived sodium hyaluronate at 80–110 kDa. The same supplier’s LMW jar, as the serum file states, is about 0.8–1.0 million daltons. That is a different polymer size. Accept the molecular-weight range on the COA. Do not store a homemade 1% gel without a preservative; the plan already says the solution is made in the batch.

Niacinamide is nicotinamide. USP is the monograph that matters. Niacin (nicotinic acid) flushes and is the wrong molecule. Tablets bring fillers. Accept USP powder.

Allantoin is the powder, poorly soluble, capped at 0.5% in the serum because it crystallizes. Accept USP or cosmetic allantoin. Reject urea.

N-acetyl-D-glucosamine is not glucosamine sulfate and not glucosamine hydrochloride. The cart’s jar is from crustacean shells, so a shellfish line on the COA belongs with that lot. A fermentation grade can be the same molecule and must still say N-acetyl glucosamine. Accept the acetylated sugar. Reject the unacetylated salts.

### Crystal Clear Wax, HiLite Copper, Sparkle Plenty

Crystal Clear Wax is the suspending wax in the shimmer spray at 0.5%. Beeswax, candelilla, and carnauba are other waxes and are not this ingredient. Accept the trade name or the INCI printed for that sample.

HiLite Copper is a fine mica colored with iron oxides, stirred into oil. The plan records Marie’s size range of 10–60 microns and notes that the seller’s page did not repeat it. Confirm particle size on the lot. Coarse sparkle clogs the mister. 21 CFR 73.1496 limits mica color-additive impurities to lead not more than 20 ppm, arsenic not more than 3 ppm, and mercury not more than 1 ppm. FDA’s public cosmetics page cites a draft guidance recommending not more than 10 ppm lead in externally applied cosmetics. Ask the pigment house for a heavy-metal statement. Plastic glitter and metal-film glitter are rejects.

Sparkle Plenty is synthetic fluorphlogopite, about 40–200 microns, plastic-free and metal-free. The CIR final assessment concluded it is safe in the practices of use and concentration reviewed, including incidental inhalation from pump sprays at ordinary cosmetic levels (CIR report; International Journal of Toxicology, 2015). It is too coarse for the perfume atomizer and for the shimmer mister. Accept the INCI and a metal statement. Reject PET glitter. Dry powder stays lidded. The quality risk is the wrong particle and the wrong mineral, not spoilage. Mica does not go rancid. A bulk sack does not get better with age, and it makes a dust the formulas tell you not to breathe.

### Iota carrageenan

FAO’s carrageenan note assigns *Eucheuma denticulatum* to iota and *Kappaphycus alvarezii* to kappa. Iota gels are soft and elastic with calcium. Kappa gels are rigid and brittle with potassium. A jar of carrageenan plus maltodextrin and dextrose is a different ingredient, which is why the Modernist Pantry-style blend stays off the cart. Accept a spec that says iota and lists no carrier. Food-grade refined iota is enough. Kappa is the false economy.

### Glycerin

USP glycerin is 1,2,3-propanetriol. Identification in the monograph includes a gas-chromatography limit of diethylene glycol and ethylene glycol at not more than 0.10% each. USP revised that test after FDA’s guidance on glycerin and diethylene glycol; the revision bulletin became official on 1 May 2009. Diethylene glycol in glycerin has killed people when industrial lots entered drug and syrup supply chains. Technical glycerin and a melt-and-pour “glycerin soap” are rejects. Accept USP, vegetable, with those two glycol results. A grocery or pharmacy USP bottle is the grade. A 55-gallon drum is not a quality upgrade for 13 g of use.

### Washing soda, vinegar, borax, sodium percarbonate, Epsom salt, castile, distilled water, wool

Super Washing Soda is sodium carbonate. Baking soda is sodium bicarbonate and will not do the dish brick’s job. A damp box has absorbed water; the grams of carbonate per scoop fall. Commodity grocery carbonate is the right grade. Pool-store soda ash can be the same chemical and a worse label. Prefer the box that says sodium carbonate.

Distilled white vinegar at about 5% acidity is the rinse. Cleaning vinegar is often 6% and is a different dose. Apple cider vinegar is a different smell.

Borax is sodium tetraborate decahydrate, for one labeled test bag, not the everyday wash. Boric acid is a different substance. A “borax substitute” is often washing soda and does not belong in that test. A pharmaceutical grade does not make the test a better idea.

Sodium percarbonate is the oxygen bleach. It is not chlorine bleach. A scented oxi powder that also contains detergent and fragrance fights the scent work and the unscented-wash rule. Moisture spends the peroxide; the powder left behind is basically washing soda and will not fizz. Buy a small unscented box when a rag wash needs it, and keep it dry.

Epsom salt is magnesium sulfate for a closet sachet. Unscented and undyed is the whole spec. USP is fine and not necessary.

Castile here means the unscented baby liquid soap already named, a potassium vegetable soap, not a sulfate detergent. Marketplace castile is a relabel risk. Read the ingredient list.

Distilled water means the jug says distilled. Spring water and “purified” drinking water still carry minerals that these water formulas are written to avoid.

Wool dryer balls are wool, unscented. A perfume coating is a second fragrance in the dryer.

### Beeswax, cetearyl alcohol, cetyl alcohol, stearic acid, emulsifying wax

White beeswax NF is the National Formulary grade, bleached or filtered so the honey color and honey smell stay out of a 10 g perfume tin. Yellow beeswax is a different color and a different smell. Paraffin and stearic acid are the classic cuts; they fail a beeswax saponification test because paraffin does not saponify. Accept white NF pastilles from a cosmetic supplier. Four tins need 8 g. A craft-store cake of unknown wax is the false economy.

Cetearyl alcohol NF, cetyl alcohol, triple-pressed stearic acid, and NF emulsifying wax are ordinary cosmetic ingredients. The failures are identity swaps: an ethoxylate instead of the alcohol, BTMS instead of NF emulsifying wax, a surfactant instead of stearic acid. Accept the INCI and the NF line where the cart asks for NF. They do not reward boutique pricing.

## 4. Receiving checklist

Use this on any box. If a line fails, do not pour the new lot into an old bottle.

**Papers**

1. The lot or batch on the COA is the lot on the container.
2. The Latin name or the INCI matches the master table, including the grade word: Maillette, complete, FCF, distilled, FFPA, rectified, Java, Sumatra, serrata, spicatum, *motia*, bigarade, bud, dark, golden, USP, NF, iota, 1,3-propanediol, ELMW, fine powder, D5.
3. If the material is not neat, the percent and the solvent are printed (10% labdanum, 10% jasmine in fractionated coconut, 10% birch tar in benzyl benzoate, 5% amber).
4. Essential oils and CO2 extracts: a lot GC-MS or GC-FID. For lavender, bergamot, sandalwood, palmarosa, and the other ISO oils, the seller says which standard the lot was checked against.
5. Bergamot FCF and distilled lemon: a bergapten result, HPLC, ISO 7358 or the seller’s equivalent.
6. Bitter almond: the words “prussic acid removed” or FFPA on the lot paper.
7. Birch tar: “rectified” and a PAH statement.
8. Aroma chemicals and fragrance oils: SDS, current IFRA certificate, and a phthalate statement. Strength stated.
9. Glycerin: USP, with diethylene glycol and ethylene glycol each ≤ 0.10%.
10. Polysorbate 20: 1,4-dioxane result (USP ceiling 10 ppm) and the INCI “polysorbate 20.”
11. Surfactants: percent active. SCI form is fine powder. CAPB includes a DMAPA / amidoamine note.
12. Pigments: heavy metals and particle size. Sparkle Plenty reads synthetic fluorphlogopite. HiLite reads a fine mica, not a plastic.
13. Carrier oils and citrus: peroxide value or a pack date you can still trust.
14. Argan: unroasted or cosmetic, acidity, peroxide value. For a suspiciously cheap bulk lot, campesterol in the sterols.
15. Reject a COA with no lot, a chromatogram that is a photograph of someone else’s chart, an ISO 9001 logo standing in for an assay, and the phrase “therapeutic grade” as the only quality claim.

**Look and smell, at the bench**

1. Seal intact. No leak. Citrus, carriers, vanilla, and the quinone jar belong in the dark.
2. Blotter the scent materials. Lavender should not smell of camphor wood. Geranium should not smell of citronella. Atlas cedar should not smell like pencils. Virginia cedar should. Peppermint should not smell like spearmint. FFPA almond should smell of almond and is still not a food.
3. Oxidized citrus smells of paint, varnish, or sharp turpentine. Do not use it on skin.
4. Grapeseed, argan, and jojoba that smell of paint, cardboard, or old nuts are past their peroxide budget.
5. Golden jojoba is gold, not water-white. Cosmetic argan is pale and does not smell of roasted nuts. Fractionated coconut barely smells. Whole coconut oil smells of coconut and goes solid when cool.
6. Glycerin is clear, thick, and sweet. A soap base smells of soap and may be a solid.
7. Polysorbate 20 is clear to pale yellow. A dark, sharp bottle is oxidized.
8. CAPB should not smell of fish.
9. SCI is a fine powder, not noodles. SCS and SLSa should be powdery, not a damp cake.
10. Washing soda is the sodium carbonate box, not the baking-soda box. Sodium percarbonate fizzes in warm water. A silent powder has already given up its oxygen.
11. Vinegar is labeled at about 5%. Distilled water says distilled.
12. HiLite in a spoon looks like a fine copper powder. Sparkle Plenty looks like snow glitter and does not look like punched plastic film. If a mister chokes, the particle is coarser than the bottle it was bought for.
13. Rose hydrosol is watery. A cologne blast means fragrance, not a distillate.
14. Stop there. Do not build a still, a chromatography column, or a cyanide test at home. The ferrous-salt test in the 1991 bitter-almond article is a factory control, not a kitchen method.

**Storage that decides whether a bulk deal stays good**

Keep expressed citrus, grapeseed, argan, jojoba, and polysorbate cool, dark, and in a small bottle. A liter of orange or a gallon of grapeseed is worse than a fresh small bottle by the time the pantry finishes it. Sodium percarbonate and washing soda stay dry. Hydrosol stays small and, if unpreserved, short-dated. Perfume absolutes and vanilla are already sized at 1 ml because that is the quality unit. Mica, wax, and USP powders do not improve in a larger sack.

## 5. Where the money belongs

**Paying more buys a real difference**

- Rose by-absolute, jasmine sambac, vanilla CO2, tonka, and *Santalum spicatum*. Cheap versions of these are usually something else.
- Bergamot FCF and distilled lemon. The cheaper expressed oils are the phototoxic grades.
- FFPA bitter almond, and only that grade. Rectified birch tar at 10% with a PAH statement. Veramoss rather than raw oakmoss.
- Palmarosa var. *motia*, lavender Maillette, ylang complete, Java vetiver, dark patchouli, Sumatra benzoin, serrata CO2, hinoki wood, and the two cedars as two species. The cheaper bottle is often a different plant or a different fraction.
- Named aroma chemicals from a perfumery house, with IFRA and SDS, neat or with the dilution printed. A marketplace drum is the adulteration, not a discount.
- USP glycerin with the diethylene glycol limit. Technical glycerin is a safety failure, not a savings.
- LC995 with a D4 number, ELMW hyaluronic acid with a dalton range, 1,3-propanediol rather than propylene glycol, N-acetyl glucosamine rather than glucosamine sulfate, iota rather than kappa or a maltodextrin blend, and SCI fine powder rather than a fatty blend.
- Argan and golden jojoba from a seller who will show peroxide value and identity. The fraud is dilution, and the shelf-life fraud is a jug that is old before it is opened.
- Pigments with a heavy-metal statement and the right particle. Plastic glitter is not a cheaper mica.

**Commodity grade is enough**

- Sodium carbonate, distilled water, 5% white vinegar, plain Epsom salt, unscented sodium percarbonate, and household borax for the one labeled test. Read the chemical name. Do not buy a premium brand.
- Superfine white rice flour or rice starch from a grocer.
- Refined grapeseed, refined shea, and refined kokum from a cosmetic supplier, fresh, with a peroxide value. A rare origin grade is not the formula.
- Fractionated coconut as caprylic/capric triglyceride. Odorless matters. A ceremonial organic seal matters less.
- SLSa, CAPB, sodium coco sulfate, and caprylyl/capryl glucoside at ordinary cosmetic prices, once the INCI and the percent active are right.
- NF or USP powders: niacinamide, allantoin, panthenol, white beeswax, cetearyl alcohol, cetyl alcohol, stearic acid, NF emulsifying wax, vanillin, ethyl maltol. USP is the quality. A skincare-brand jar of the same powder is not purer.
- Mixed tocopherols T-50 from a cosmetic supplier. The acetate is not a cheaper vitamin E for these oils.
- Dimethicone 350 and C12-15 alkyl benzoate as stated viscosities and INCIs.
- Unscented wool dryer balls, and an unscented liquid soap whose label you can read.

**A higher price that does not buy quality**

- Organic in place of FCF, in place of distilled lemon, or in place of ylang complete.
- Ylang Extra, Hedione HC, clear jojoba, unrefined smoky shea, and a 15 ml of *Santalum album* when the formula asked for something else.
- ISO 9001, “therapeutic grade,” and a bulk COA that is not tied to the lot.
- Gallons of citrus, carrier oil, polysorbate, hydrosol, or ethanol. The large size is how a good material stops being good.
